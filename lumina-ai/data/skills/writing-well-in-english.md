# Writing Well in English

English is a global lingua franca prized for **clarity and directness**. Good English writing isn't fancy — it's clear, concise, and natural. The tradition (Strunk & White, plain-English movement) values getting meaning across efficiently. For non-native writers, a handful of tricky features (articles, prepositions, tense) are the main hurdles between correct and *natural* English.

## Clarity & Concision (The Core Values)

English writing culture prizes **plain, clear, concise** prose:
- **Omit needless words** — cut redundancy, filler ("very," "really," "in order to"→"to"), and throat-clearing. Say it in fewer words.
- **Prefer plain words** over jargon and inflated vocabulary ("use" not "utilize," "help" not "facilitate"). Simple is strong.
- **One idea per sentence**, clear structure, short-to-medium sentences (vary length for rhythm — see prose-style-and-voice). Clarity beats cleverness.

## Active Voice

English strongly favors the **active voice** ("The team shipped the feature") over passive ("The feature was shipped by the team") — it's clearer, shorter, and identifies who did what. Use passive deliberately (when the actor is unknown/unimportant), not by default. Overusing passive is the #1 marker of stiff, bureaucratic English.

## Register: Formal vs Casual

- **Formal** — full words (no contractions), precise vocabulary, complete sentences, impersonal; for business, academic, official writing.
- **Casual** — contractions (don't, it's), simpler words, conversational, personal; for emails, chat, blogs.
- English politeness leans on **hedging and indirectness** ("Could you possibly…", "I was wondering if…", "It might be worth…") especially in requests — directness can read as rude in some contexts. Match register to audience.

## Common ESL Pitfalls

The features non-natives most often stumble on:
- **Articles (a/an/the)** — English requires them where many languages don't; a/an (indefinite, first mention) vs the (definite, specific/known) vs none (general plurals/uncountables). Genuinely hard; a top tell of non-native writing.
- **Prepositions** — often idiomatic and unpredictable ("interested *in*," "good *at*," "depend *on*," "arrive *at/in*"). Learned case by case.
- **Tense and aspect** — the present perfect ("I have done") vs simple past ("I did"), continuous forms, and sequence-of-tenses trip up learners.
- **Countable/uncountable nouns** ("informations," "advices" are wrong), subject-verb agreement, and word order in questions.

Awareness of these catches most non-native errors.

## Idiom & Natural Phrasing

- **Collocations** — words that naturally go together ("make a decision" not "do a decision," "heavy rain" not "strong rain"). Getting collocations right is much of what makes English sound native.
- **Idioms and phrasal verbs** — "give up," "look into," "put off" — pervasive and often unpredictable; a rich but tricky layer.
- **Natural phrasing** — avoid translated stiffness; write how English speakers actually put things. Read aloud; if it sounds "off," it probably is.

## British vs American

Differences in spelling (colour/color, -ise/-ize, centre/center), vocabulary (lift/elevator, flat/apartment), some grammar, and date formats. **Pick one and be consistent** for your audience.

Write English well by prioritizing **clarity and concision** (cut needless words, plain vocabulary), favoring the **active voice**, matching **register** (formal/casual, with appropriate hedging for politeness), watching the classic **ESL pitfalls** (articles, prepositions, tense), using natural **idiom and collocations**, and keeping **British/American conventions consistent**. Good English is clear, direct, and natural — not ornate. Write to be understood effortlessly.
