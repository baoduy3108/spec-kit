# Point of View & Narration

Point of view (POV) is **who tells the story and through whose consciousness the reader experiences it.** It's one of the most fundamental (and quietly powerful) craft choices — it shapes intimacy, information, voice, and trust. Choosing and controlling POV well is invisible when right and jarring when wrong.

## The Persons

- **First person ("I")** — the narrator is a character telling their own story. Intimate and immediate; strong voice; but limited to what that character knows, sees, and perceives (and colored by their biases). Great for deep interiority and voice-driven fiction.
- **Second person ("You")** — rare; addresses the reader as "you." Distinctive, immersive or unsettling, hard to sustain. Used for specific effects (see interactive fiction).
- **Third person ("he/she/they")** — the default of much fiction; a narrator outside the characters. Comes in flavors of *closeness* (below), balancing intimacy and flexibility.

## Limited vs Omniscient (Third Person)

The key third-person distinction is **how much the narrator knows/accesses**:
- **Third limited** — the narrator sticks to *one* character's perspective (their thoughts, perceptions, knowledge) per scene/chapter. Like first person's intimacy but with a bit more flexibility. The most common modern choice. Multi-POV novels use limited but switch characters (cleanly, at breaks).
- **Third omniscient** — an all-knowing narrator who can access *any* character's thoughts, know things no character knows, and comment. Broad scope and authorial voice, but more distant; harder to do well without feeling detached or "head-hopping."

## Narrative Distance

A subtle, powerful dial: **how close the narration sits to the character's consciousness** — from far (a distant, reporting narrator: "The woman entered the house.") to deep (fully inside the character's head, their voice bleeding into the narration: "God, not this house again."). Skilled writers *slide* the distance — pulling back for overview, moving in tight for emotional intensity. Controlling narrative distance shapes intimacy moment to moment, even within a single POV.

## Head-Hopping (The Common Error)

**Head-hopping** — carelessly jumping between characters' interior thoughts *within a single scene* in limited POV — disorients readers (whose head am I in?) and breaks intimacy. In third limited, stay in *one* head per scene; switch only at clear breaks (chapter/section). Omniscient *can* move between minds, but needs a controlled, consistent narratorial hand, not random hopping. Head-hopping is a hallmark of unpolished prose.

## Unreliable Narrators

A narrator the reader can't fully trust — because they lie, are deceived, biased, limited, or mad. Powerful for mystery, irony, and depth: the gap between what the narrator says and what's *true* engages the reader as detective. Requires careful seeding of clues so the reader can sense the unreliability. A sophisticated technique (first person especially).

## Tense

- **Past tense** — the default; feels natural, established, and flexible.
- **Present tense** — immediate, urgent, "happening now"; increasingly common (esp. YA, literary), but can feel gimmicky if unmotivated and is harder to sustain. Choose deliberately, and be consistent.

## Matching POV to the Story

POV isn't arbitrary — choose based on what the story needs:
- **Intimacy and voice** → first person or close third.
- **A single strong character lens** → first or third limited.
- **Multiple perspectives / broad scope** → multi-POV third limited or omniscient.
- **Mystery / withholding** → limited (control what the reader knows via what the POV character knows); unreliability for twists.
- **Irony, scope, authorial commentary** → omniscient.
- **What information must be hidden or revealed** is often *the* deciding factor — POV controls what the reader can know.

**Consistency** is essential — establish the POV rules and honor them; violating them (a limited narrator suddenly knowing another's thoughts) breaks the reader's trust and immersion.

Choose point of view by matching **person (first/second/third)** and **access (limited/omniscient)** to the story's needs for intimacy, scope, and information control, mastering **narrative distance** to modulate closeness, avoiding **head-hopping** (one head per scene in limited), wielding **unreliable narration** and **tense** deliberately, and staying **consistent**. POV is the lens through which readers experience everything — choose it to serve the story's intimacy, mystery, and voice, then hold it steady.
