# The Scientific Method & Experimentation

The scientific method is the systematic process by which science **builds reliable knowledge** — through observation, hypothesis, experiment, and evidence. Understanding it is essential not just for doing science but for evaluating claims critically in any field.

## The Process

The classic cycle:
1. **Observation & question** — notice something, ask why.
2. **Hypothesis** — a *testable, falsifiable* proposed explanation ("if... then..."). A good hypothesis makes predictions and *can be proven wrong*.
3. **Experiment** — a controlled test of the hypothesis's predictions.
4. **Data collection & analysis** — gather and interpret results.
5. **Conclusion** — does the evidence support or refute the hypothesis? Refine and repeat.
Science is **iterative** — hypotheses are continually tested, revised, or discarded.

## Variables & Controls

Rigorous experiments isolate cause and effect:
- **Independent variable** — what you deliberately change (the suspected cause).
- **Dependent variable** — what you measure (the effect).
- **Controlled variables** — everything else kept constant, so only the independent variable can explain changes.
- **Control group** — a comparison that doesn't receive the treatment (vs the experimental group). Isolates the treatment's effect (e.g. a placebo in medicine).

Without controls, you can't attribute an effect to a cause.

## Experimental Design

- **Fair test** — change one variable at a time; keep others constant.
- **Sample size** — larger samples give more reliable results (reduce the role of chance).
- **Randomization and blinding** — reduce bias (see cognitive-biases); double-blind trials are the gold standard in medicine.
- **Repeatability** — repeat trials to check consistency and reduce random error.

## Data Collection & Analysis

- **Accurate, precise measurement** — accuracy (closeness to true value) vs precision (consistency); minimize systematic and random errors.
- **Analysis** — use statistics (see statistics-fundamentals) to find patterns, averages, and whether results are **significant** (unlikely to be chance) — not just eyeballing.
- **Graphs and tables** to present data clearly (see data-visualization).
- **Correlation ≠ causation** — a key caution: an association doesn't prove one thing causes another.

## Drawing Conclusions

- **Evidence-based** — conclusions must follow from the data, acknowledging uncertainty and limitations.
- **Support or refute** — a hypothesis is supported (never absolutely "proven") or refuted; even supported ones remain provisional.
- **Avoid overreach** — don't claim more than the data show.

## The Nature of Scientific Knowledge

- **Theory** — in science, a well-substantiated, extensively-tested *explanation* (evolution, gravity, germ theory) — not a guess. The highest status of scientific knowledge.
- **Falsifiability** — scientific claims must be testable and potentially disprovable (Popper). Unfalsifiable claims aren't science.
- **Peer review and reproducibility** — results are scrutinized by other experts and must be **repeatable** by others to be trusted. Self-correcting over time.
- **Tentative but reliable** — science is always open to revision with new evidence, yet this openness is its strength, producing the most reliable knowledge we have.

Master the scientific method via the **iterative process** (observation → testable hypothesis → experiment → analysis → conclusion), rigorous use of **variables and controls** (independent/dependent/controlled, control groups), sound **experimental design** (fair tests, sample size, randomization, blinding, repetition), careful **data analysis** (accuracy/precision, statistics, correlation ≠ causation), **evidence-based conclusions** (provisional, not overreaching), and the **nature of scientific knowledge** (theories as tested explanations, falsifiability, peer review, reproducibility). This is how humanity distinguishes reliable knowledge from mere belief — a tool for science and for critical thinking everywhere.
