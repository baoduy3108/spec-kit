# Writing Compelling Dialogue

Dialogue is often what makes fiction come alive — it reveals character, advances plot, and creates immediacy. But good fictional dialogue isn't transcribed real speech (which is full of "um," repetition, and boring pleasantries). It's a **crafted illusion** of real talk: sharper, purposeful, and layered. Great dialogue is a distinct skill, and stiff dialogue kills otherwise-good writing.

## Distinct Character Voices

Each character should **sound different** (see character-development-and-arc):
- **Word choice, rhythm, vocabulary, grammar, attitude** — shaped by their background, education, personality, mood. A gruff soldier, a nervous academic, and a street kid don't talk alike.
- **The test**: cover the dialogue tags — can you tell who's speaking? If everyone sounds like the author, the voices are flat.
- Voice reveals character *without narration* — how someone speaks *is* characterization.

## Subtext: What's Unsaid

The most important principle: **people rarely say exactly what they mean.** Great dialogue runs on **subtext** — the real meaning beneath the words:
- Characters evade, hint, deflect, lie, and talk around things. What's *not* said, and *why*, carries the emotional weight.
- Two people arguing about the dishes may really be arguing about respect. The surface conversation and the true conflict differ.
- Subtext trusts the reader to *read between the lines*, which is far more engaging than characters baldly stating feelings. On-the-nose dialogue ("I'm angry because you betrayed me and I feel unloved") is dead; subtext is alive.

## Dialogue Does Double Duty

Every line should **do more than one job** — ideally reveal *character* AND advance *plot* AND carry *subtext/tension* simultaneously. Dialogue that only conveys information (or only fills space) is wasted. The best lines are efficient: they characterize, move the story, and resonate at once. Cut dialogue that does only one small thing.

## Avoid the On-the-Nose & the Infodump

Two classic failures:
- **On-the-nose** — characters saying exactly, literally what they feel/mean with no subtext. Flat and unrealistic.
- **"As you know, Bob" / expository dialogue** — characters telling each other things they both already know, purely to inform the reader ("As you know, our father, the king, died last spring…"). It's transparent and clunky. Convey backstory/info through *conflict, implication, and need* — or elsewhere — not lectures disguised as talk.

## Conflict & Tension

Compelling dialogue usually contains **tension or conflict** — even friendly scenes have undercurrents (competing goals, discomfort, things unsaid). Characters *want* different things in a conversation, creating friction. Pure agreement/pleasantries are boring; give conversations a subtle push-pull, an agenda on each side. Conflict in dialogue is a mini-engine of drama.

## Tags & Beats

- **Dialogue tags** — mostly use plain "**said**" (and "asked"); it's invisible and lets the dialogue shine. Avoid distracting "said-bookisms" (exclaimed, retorted, opined) and adverb-laden tags ("she said angrily" — show the anger instead).
- **Action beats** — interspersing small actions/gestures ("He set down his cup.") attributes speech, adds physicality, controls pacing, and reveals emotion through body language (show, don't tell). Beats break up dialogue and ground it in a scene.
- **White space** — dialogue-heavy passages read fast and feel dynamic; use that pacing effect deliberately.

## Realistic But Not Real

The paradox: dialogue should *feel* natural but be **more purposeful than real speech**:
- **Cut the filler** — real conversation's ums, tangents, and dull pleasantries mostly get trimmed (keep a *flavor* for realism, not the tedium).
- **Compress and sharpen** — get to the point (or the evasion) faster than real life; every line earns its place.
- **But keep it natural** — contractions, interruptions, incomplete sentences, individual rhythms. Read it aloud — if it sounds stilted or like nobody talks that way, fix it.

Write compelling dialogue by giving each character a **distinct voice**, running conversations on **subtext** (what's unsaid), making every line **do double duty** (character + plot + tension), avoiding **on-the-nose and expository** talk, seeding **conflict** into exchanges, using mostly invisible **"said" tags with action beats**, and crafting speech that's **realistic but sharper than real** — read aloud to test. Great dialogue is a duel of intentions beneath the words, revealing who people are through how, and what, they choose to say.
