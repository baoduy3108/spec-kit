# Organic Reaction Mechanisms (Advanced Chemistry)

Beyond memorizing organic reactions (see organic-chemistry-basics), **mechanisms** explain *how* and *why* they happen — the step-by-step movement of electrons as bonds break and form. Understanding mechanisms lets you predict products and reactivity rather than memorize, a hallmark of advanced/gifted-exam (chuyên) chemistry.

## Bond Breaking & Forming

- **Homolytic cleavage** — a bond breaks evenly, each atom keeping one electron → **radicals** (unpaired electrons). Common in high-energy/light-driven reactions (halogenation of alkanes).
- **Heterolytic cleavage** — a bond breaks unevenly, one atom takes both electrons → **ions** (a cation and anion). The basis of most polar organic mechanisms.
- **Arrow-pushing** — curved arrows show electron-pair movement (from source of electrons to where the new bond forms). The language of mechanisms.

## Nucleophiles & Electrophiles

The central actors:
- **Nucleophile** ("nucleus-loving") — electron-rich, donates a pair to form a bond (OH⁻, NH₃, anything with lone pairs or π electrons).
- **Electrophile** ("electron-loving") — electron-poor, accepts a pair (carbocations, carbonyl carbons, H⁺).
- **Reactions are nucleophiles attacking electrophiles** — electron-rich meets electron-poor. Identifying which is which predicts the reaction.

## Substitution (SN1 / SN2)

A nucleophile replaces a leaving group:
- **SN2** — one step, the nucleophile attacks as the leaving group departs (backside attack, inversion of configuration). Rate depends on *both* nucleophile and substrate (2nd order); favored by primary carbons, strong nucleophiles.
- **SN1** — two steps: the leaving group departs first (forming a **carbocation**), then the nucleophile attacks. Rate depends only on the substrate (1st order); favored by tertiary carbons (stable carbocation), weak nucleophiles.
- **Carbocation stability** (3° > 2° > 1°) governs which pathway dominates.

## Elimination (E1 / E2)

Forming a double bond by removing atoms:
- **E2** — one step, base removes a proton as the leaving group departs (concerted). 2nd order.
- **E1** — two steps via a carbocation (like SN1). 1st order.
- **Substitution vs elimination compete** — conditions (base strength, temperature, substrate) determine which wins. Zaitsev's rule: the more substituted alkene usually predominates.

## Addition to Double Bonds

Alkenes/alkynes (electron-rich π bonds) react with electrophiles:
- **Electrophilic addition** — the π electrons attack an electrophile, opening the double bond (adding H-X, halogens, water).
- **Markovnikov's rule** — in adding H-X to an unsymmetrical alkene, H goes to the carbon with more H's (the more stable carbocation forms), so X adds to the more substituted carbon.

## Why Mechanisms Matter

- **Predicting products** — from the mechanism you deduce what forms, including regiochemistry (Markovnikov) and stereochemistry (SN2 inversion).
- **Reactivity trends** — carbocation stability, nucleophile/base strength, and leaving-group ability explain rates and pathways.
- **Reaction intermediates** — carbocations, carbanions, radicals, and their relative stabilities are the key to reasoning.

Master organic mechanisms via **bond cleavage** (homolytic→radicals, heterolytic→ions, arrow-pushing), the **nucleophile-electrophile** framework (electron-rich attacks electron-poor), **substitution** (SN1 via carbocation vs SN2 concerted inversion), **elimination** (E1/E2, competing with substitution), and **electrophilic addition** (Markovnikov via the stable carbocation). Reasoning from electron movement and intermediate stability — rather than memorizing — is exactly the mechanistic depth that earns top marks in gifted-exam organic chemistry.
