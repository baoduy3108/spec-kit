# Writing Well in Japanese

Japanese is famous for two things that dominate writing well: a **three-script writing system** and **keigo** — an elaborate system of politeness levels woven into the grammar itself. Getting the politeness level right for the social situation is arguably *more* important than in almost any other language, and it's encoded in the verbs and vocabulary you choose.

## The Three Scripts

Japanese mixes **three writing systems** in normal text:
- **Kanji (漢字)** — Chinese characters for content words (nouns, verb/adjective stems); carry meaning compactly.
- **Hiragana (ひらがな)** — a phonetic syllabary for grammar (particles, inflections, native words without kanji).
- **Katakana (カタカナ)** — a phonetic syllabary for loanwords (コーヒー coffee), foreign names, onomatopoeia, and emphasis.

Good writing uses the right script for each element and an appropriate **kanji level** (too many kanji is dense; too few is childish). Balance is a literacy marker.

## Keigo: Politeness Levels (The Core Skill)

Japanese grammatically encodes **social hierarchy and politeness** through **keigo (敬語)**:
- **Plain form (だ/する)** — casual; close friends, family, informal writing.
- **Polite form (です/ます)** — the default respectful register; strangers, general writing.
- **Honorific (尊敬語)** — elevates the *other* person (their actions); for superiors/customers.
- **Humble (謙譲語)** — lowers *yourself* (your actions) to show respect.

Choosing the right level for the relationship (age, status, in-group/out-group, customer vs colleague) is essential and unavoidable — using plain form with a superior is rude; excessive keigo with a friend is cold or sarcastic. Keigo mastery is the heart of appropriate Japanese, and it's a lifelong skill even for natives.

## Particles & SOV Order

- **Subject-Object-Verb** — the verb comes last (私はりんごを食べる = "I apple eat"). Sentences build toward the verb.
- **Particles** mark grammatical roles: は (topic), が (subject), を (object), に (direction/time), で (location/means), へ, と, から, まで, の, も. Particles do the work that word order/prepositions do in English; using the right one is fundamental.

## Wa vs Ga (Topic vs Subject)

A famous subtlety: **は (wa)** marks the **topic** ("as for X…") while **が (ga)** marks the **grammatical subject** (often introducing new/emphasized info). The wa/ga distinction is one of the hardest nuances — it shifts emphasis and nuance subtly. Getting it right is a mark of fluency.

## Formality & Context

- **Register** spans casual speech, polite writing, formal/business (heavy keigo), and literary styles. Written Japanese has its own conventions (formal endings like である for essays).
- **Implicitness / context** — Japanese omits what's understood: **subjects and pronouns are frequently dropped** (understood from context and keigo). Over-stating "I/you" (私/あなた) sounds unnatural and even rude; the language prefers implication. High-context communication — much is left unsaid and inferred.
- **Indirectness and softening** — politeness favors indirect, softened phrasing; blunt directness can be impolite.

Write Japanese well by balancing the **three scripts** with appropriate kanji level, above all choosing the correct **keigo politeness level** for the social relationship (the central skill), using **particles** correctly in **SOV** order, handling the subtle **wa/ga** topic-subject distinction, and embracing Japanese **implicitness** (drop understood subjects/pronouns, prefer indirect softened phrasing). The soul of good Japanese is respectful, context-aware, appropriately-polite expression — saying the right thing at the right level for whom you're addressing.
