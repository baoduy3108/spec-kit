# Social Psychology Fundamentals

Social psychology studies how people's thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are **influenced by others** — real, imagined, or implied. Its most profound lesson is the **power of the situation**: we vastly overestimate personality and underestimate context in explaining behavior. Understanding social influence illuminates persuasion, group dynamics, and much of human behavior.

## The Power of the Situation

The field's central insight: **situations shape behavior far more than we think**:
- Classic studies (Milgram's obedience, Zimbardo's prison, Asch's conformity) showed ordinary people doing extraordinary things under situational pressure.
- **Fundamental attribution error** — we attribute others' behavior to their *character* ("he's rude") while attributing our own to *circumstances* ("I was stressed"). We systematically under-weight the situation.
- This reframing breeds humility and better analysis: to understand or change behavior, look at the *system and context*, not just the person.

## Conformity & Obedience

- **Conformity** — adjusting behavior/beliefs to match a group. People conform for two reasons: **informational** (others may know better) and **normative** (to fit in / avoid rejection). Asch showed people deny obvious truths to match a unanimous group.
- **Obedience** — following authority. Milgram showed most people would (they believed) harm another when instructed by an authority figure. Situational factors (proximity, legitimacy) strongly modulate it.
- **Groupthink** — cohesive groups suppress dissent for harmony, producing bad decisions (counter with diverse input, devil's advocates — see decision-making).

## Persuasion & Influence (Cialdini's Principles)

Cialdini's six (+1) principles of influence — the levers of persuasion:
- **Reciprocity** — we feel obliged to return favors (a free sample creates obligation).
- **Commitment & consistency** — once we commit (especially publicly), we act consistently with it (foot-in-the-door).
- **Social proof** — we look to others' behavior to decide our own, especially under uncertainty ("best-seller," "most popular").
- **Authority** — we defer to credible experts and authority symbols.
- **Liking** — we're persuaded by people we like (similar, attractive, complimentary).
- **Scarcity** — we value what's rare or fleeting ("limited time," "only 3 left").
- **Unity** — shared identity ("one of us").

These are powerful and widely used in marketing/sales — and, like nudges, carry ethical weight (persuasion vs manipulation).

## Cognitive Dissonance

**Dissonance** — the discomfort of holding conflicting beliefs, or of behavior clashing with beliefs. We reduce it by changing attitudes to match actions ("I did it, so I must believe in it") — which is why small commitments change self-perception, and why we rationalize choices after making them. A powerful engine of attitude change and self-justification.

## Attribution & Perception

- **Attribution** — how we explain behavior (internal/dispositional vs external/situational). Biased by the fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias (crediting ourselves for success, blaming circumstances for failure).
- **Stereotypes and schemas** — mental shortcuts for social categories that speed perception but cause bias and error.

## In-Group / Out-Group Dynamics

- We readily divide into **in-groups ("us")** and **out-groups ("them")** — even on trivial bases (minimal group paradigm). This drives favoritism, prejudice, and intergroup conflict.
- **In-group bias** (favoring "us") and **out-group homogeneity** (seeing "them" as all alike) are pervasive. Understanding this is key to prejudice, polarization, and building cohesion or division.

Reason about social behavior via the **power of the situation** (context over character; fundamental attribution error), **conformity and obedience** (informational/normative pressure, authority), **persuasion principles** (reciprocity, social proof, scarcity, authority, liking, commitment, unity), **cognitive dissonance** (we rationalize to align beliefs and actions), **attribution** biases, and **in-group/out-group** dynamics. The core lesson: humans are profoundly shaped by their social context — to understand people, examine the situation, not just the person.
