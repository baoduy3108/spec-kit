# Literary Analysis & Criticism

Literary analysis is the skill of **interpreting a text closely** — examining *how* a work creates meaning and effect, not just *what* happens. It's central to literature study (chuyên Văn) and to writing insightful essays about poems, stories, and novels. The goal is a supported *interpretation*, not a summary.

## Elements to Analyze

- **Theme** — the central ideas or messages a work explores (love, power, mortality, justice). The deepest layer; analysis aims to uncover and interpret themes.
- **Character** — motivations, development/arcs, relationships, and what characters represent (see character-development-and-arc).
- **Plot & structure** — how events are arranged, conflict, turning points, and pacing for effect (see story-structure-and-plot).
- **Setting** — time, place, and atmosphere, and how they shape meaning and mood.
- **Point of view** — who narrates and how it shapes our understanding (see point-of-view-and-narration).

## Literary Devices

The *techniques* authors use to create meaning and effect — what analysis examines closely:
- **Metaphor & simile** — comparisons that illuminate (and carry thematic weight).
- **Symbolism** — objects/images standing for larger ideas (a river = time, a wall = division).
- **Imagery** — sensory language creating vivid pictures and mood.
- **Irony** — a gap between appearance and reality (verbal, situational, dramatic).
- **Foreshadowing, motif, allusion, personification, tone shifts, diction** — and more.
Analysis identifies devices *and explains their effect and contribution to meaning* — never just labels them.

## Tone & Style

- **Tone** — the author's/narrator's attitude toward the subject (ironic, elegiac, celebratory), conveyed through word choice and detail.
- **Style** — the distinctive *how* of the writing: diction, syntax, imagery, rhythm (see prose-style-and-voice). How style shapes experience is a rich analytical vein.

## Interpretation (The Core Skill)

- **Close reading** — attending to specific words, images, and structures, and asking *why* the author chose them and what effect they create.
- **Making a claim** — a strong analysis advances an **interpretation** (a thesis about meaning) and **supports it with evidence** from the text (quotes, details), explaining how the evidence proves the point.
- **Beyond the literal** — literature works through connotation, ambiguity, and subtext; good analysis reads *into* the text (justified by evidence), exploring multiple layers.
- **Context** — historical, cultural, biographical, and genre context can deepen interpretation (critical lenses: historical, feminist, psychological, etc.).

## Writing the Analytical Essay

- **Thesis** — a clear, arguable interpretive claim (not "the poem is about love" but *how* and *to what effect*).
- **Structure** — organized paragraphs, each developing one point with textual evidence and analysis (the "quote → explain → connect to thesis" pattern).
- **Evidence and explanation** — integrate quotations and *analyze* them (don't just drop them); explain how each supports your reading.
- **Insight over summary** — assume the reader knows the plot; offer *interpretation*.

Do literary analysis by examining the **elements** (theme, character, plot, setting, POV), identifying **literary devices** and *explaining their effect on meaning*, reading **tone and style**, performing **close reading** to build a supported **interpretation** (a thesis backed by textual evidence), and writing **analytical essays** that argue an insight rather than summarize. The essence — asking *how* and *why* a text creates its meaning and effect — is the interpretive depth that literature study and chuyên Văn reward.
