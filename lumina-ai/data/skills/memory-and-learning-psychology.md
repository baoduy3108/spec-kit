# Memory & Learning Psychology

Understanding how human memory actually works reveals why common study methods (re-reading, highlighting) are weak and which techniques genuinely build durable knowledge. The science of memory and learning is well-established and often counterintuitive — the methods that feel *easy* are usually the *least* effective.

## How Memory Works

Memory involves three processes:
- **Encoding** — getting information in (attention and meaningful processing are essential; passive exposure encodes poorly).
- **Storage** — retaining it over time.
- **Retrieval** — getting it back out (the act of retrieval itself *strengthens* memory — a key insight).

And a rough architecture:
- **Sensory memory** → **working memory** (tiny capacity, ~seconds, the "mental workspace" — see cognitive-load-theory) → **long-term memory** (vast, durable). Learning is moving information into long-term memory in a retrievable form.

## The Forgetting Curve

Ebbinghaus showed memory **decays exponentially** — we forget most new information within days *unless* it's reinforced. Each successful review **flattens the curve** (slower subsequent forgetting). This is why cramming fails (steep forgetting after) and why *timing* of review is as important as amount. Forgetting is the default; durable learning fights it.

## Spaced Repetition (The Most Powerful Technique)

**Spacing** — distributing study over time (vs massing it all at once) — produces dramatically better long-term retention. Reviewing just as you're about to forget, at expanding intervals, is optimal:
- **Spaced repetition systems** (Anki, Leitner boxes) schedule reviews at increasing intervals, concentrating effort where forgetting looms. Extremely efficient for durable memory.
- **The spacing effect** is one of the most robust findings in all of psychology. Cramming feels productive but the knowledge evaporates; spacing feels harder but sticks.

## Retrieval Practice (Testing Effect)

**Actively recalling** information (self-testing, flashcards, practice problems) strengthens memory far more than **re-reading or reviewing**:
- The effort of *pulling information out* (even when you struggle or fail, then check) builds durable, retrievable memory. Recognition ("looks familiar") ≠ recall ("can produce it").
- **Testing is learning, not just assessment.** This "testing effect" is huge and underused — students vastly prefer (ineffective) re-reading.
- **Desirable difficulty** — techniques that feel harder (retrieval, spacing) produce better learning than easy ones (re-reading) that create fluent *illusions of knowing*.

## Elaboration & Interleaving

- **Elaboration** — connecting new information to what you already know, explaining *why* and *how*, generating examples. Deep, meaningful processing encodes far better than rote (the "levels of processing" effect).
- **Interleaving** — mixing different topics/problem types in a study session (vs blocking one type at a time). Feels harder and slower, but improves discrimination and transfer, especially for problem-solving.
- **Dual coding** — combining words with visuals leverages multiple memory pathways.
- **Concrete examples** and **generating** (producing an answer before being told) both strengthen learning.

## Applying It (Evidence-Based Study)

The counterintuitive takeaway: **the most effective methods feel harder and less fluent**:
- **Do**: retrieval practice (self-test), spaced review, interleaving, elaboration, teaching others. Effortful and durable.
- **Avoid over-relying on**: re-reading, highlighting, massed cramming — they feel productive (fluency illusion) but produce fragile learning.
- **Get enough sleep** — memory *consolidates* during sleep; all-nighters undermine the very learning they're meant to achieve.

Reason about learning using the **encode–store–retrieve** model and **working vs long-term memory**, respecting the **forgetting curve** (decay is default), and building durable knowledge through **spaced repetition**, **retrieval practice** (testing effect), **elaboration**, and **interleaving** — embracing *desirable difficulty* over the fluency illusion of re-reading. The methods that feel effortful are the ones that actually work.
