# Character Development & Arc

Characters are what readers *care about* — plot happens *to* people, and we follow stories for the people in them. Compelling characters have depth, want things, have flaws, make choices, and (often) change. Building them is the difference between a story readers feel and one they merely watch.

## Motivation: Want vs Need

The engine of character is **desire** — a character must *want* something, urgently. This drives their choices and the plot. The deepest characters have a distinction between:
- **Want (external goal)** — the concrete thing they're chasing (win the crown, get revenge, find the treasure). Drives the *plot*.
- **Need (internal truth)** — what they actually *need* to become whole, often something they don't realize or resist (to forgive, to trust, to accept themselves). Drives the *arc/theme*.

The tension between want and need — pursuing the goal while avoiding the growth — is where rich character stories live. Often the climax forces a choice between the want and the need.

## Flaws & Wounds

- **Flaws** — weaknesses, blind spots, vices (pride, fear, selfishness) that create internal conflict, cause mistakes, and give room to grow. Flawless characters are boring and unrelatable; flaws make them human.
- **The wound / backstory** — a past hurt that shaped the flaw and the lie the character believes about themselves/the world. The wound explains *why* they are the way they are, giving depth and a source of the arc.

Flaws and wounds create the internal problem the arc resolves.

## Character Arcs

An **arc** is how a character *changes* over the story:
- **Positive/change arc** — the character overcomes their flaw/lie and grows (the most common: they need to change, resist, and finally do). Deeply satisfying.
- **Flat/steadfast arc** — the character doesn't change but changes *the world/others* by holding to their truth (heroes who stay true and transform those around them).
- **Negative/fall arc** — the character declines, is corrupted, or fails to change (tragedy). Powerful and dark.

The arc usually intertwines with plot: external events *force* internal change (or reveal its absence). Character and plot should drive each other.

## Agency

Characters must have **agency** — make meaningful choices that drive the story, rather than passively having things happen to them. A protagonist who only reacts feels weak; one who *decides, acts, and causes consequences* engages us. Even in dire situations, the character's *choices* (however constrained) are what we follow. Agency is what makes a character a protagonist, not a passenger.

## Consistency & Believability

- **Consistency** — characters act according to their established personality, values, and logic. When they act "out of character," it must be motivated and earned, or readers feel cheated. (True growth is *change*, not inconsistency — it's set up and earned.)
- **Believable psychology** — real, understandable motivations and reactions. Even villains should make sense *to themselves* (nobody's the villain of their own story).
- **Contradiction and depth** — real people contain contradictions; a character with tensions (brave but insecure) feels three-dimensional.

## Distinct Voice

Each character should feel **individual** — a distinct **voice** (word choice, rhythm, attitude in dialogue — see writing-compelling-dialogue), mannerisms, worldview. Readers should be able to tell who's speaking without tags. Distinct voices make an ensemble come alive.

## Making Them Compelling

- **Show them through action and choice** (see show-dont-tell) — who a character *is* is revealed by what they *do* under pressure, not by description.
- **Relatable or fascinating** — readers connect through empathy (shared feelings/flaws) or intrigue. Give them something we recognize or admire or fear.
- **Let them drive** — the best characters feel like they have wills of their own, making choices that surprise even the writer.

Build characters around a burning **want masking a deeper need**, meaningful **flaws rooted in a wound**, an **arc** of change (or steadfastness, or fall), genuine **agency** in driving the story, **consistent believable psychology**, and a **distinct voice** — revealed through choices under pressure. Great characters are why readers turn pages: make them want, make them flawed, make them choose.
