# Asymmetric Multiplayer Design

Asymmetric multiplayer games pit players against each other in **fundamentally different roles** — different abilities, goals, rules, and often numbers (Dead by Daylight's 1 killer vs 4 survivors, Evolve's monster vs hunters, hidden-role games like Among Us). Instead of a mirror match, each side plays a *different game* against the other. It's exhilarating and notoriously hard to balance.

## Different Sides, Different Games

The defining feature: **sides are not equal or mirrored**:
- Each role has **distinct mechanics, objectives, powers, and win conditions.** The killer hunts; survivors evade and escape. The monster grows; hunters coordinate to take it down.
- Often **numerically asymmetric** (1-vs-many) — one powerful player against a coordinated group, each individually weaker.
- Players choose which *experience* they want (predator or prey, powerful-but-alone or teamwork-dependent). Each side is a different fantasy and skill set.

This variety is the appeal; the imbalance is the challenge.

## Balancing Unequal Roles

The hardest problem: **making unequal sides fair** — not by making them the *same*, but by making both **win-rates and fun** comparable:
- Balance around **outcomes and experience**, not symmetry. The 1 should feel powerful; the many should feel they have a real chance through coordination.
- **Team coordination vs individual power** — the lone side is strong but must do everything; the group is individually weak but multiplies through teamwork. Balancing "one skilled player vs four coordinated" across *all skill levels* (solo-queue vs premade groups) is brutally hard.
- Constant tuning; both sides will feel underpowered at times ("killer is OP" / "survivors are OP" simultaneously is common). Data-driven balance toward ~even win rates *and* mutual fun.

## Information Asymmetry

Many asymmetric games run on **unequal information** — a source of tension and mind-games:
- One side may hide (stealth, hidden identity — Among Us's imposter), the other must *find* or *deduce*. Bluffing, hunting, and hiding create psychological drama.
- Managing *what each side knows* (fog, hidden roles, tracking tools) is a core design lever and a huge tension generator.

## Power Fantasy on Both Sides

Crucial: **each role must be fun and empowering in its own way**:
- The powerful side (killer/monster) delivers a **predator power fantasy** — stalking, overpowering.
- The weaker side delivers a **tension/teamwork fantasy** — the thrill of *survival*, narrow escapes, clutch cooperation, outsmarting a stronger foe.
- Neither side should feel like a chore or a foregone conclusion. If one side isn't fun, nobody queues for it (breaking matchmaking — see below).

## Tension & Counterplay

- **Counterplay** — each side must have *answers* to the other; no unbeatable strategy. The powerful side has weaknesses to exploit; the weak side has tools and coordination.
- **Tension arc** — the cat-and-mouse builds dread and excitement (the chase, the near-escape, the reveal). Pacing this tension is central to the experience.

## Matchmaking & Role Preference

A unique operational challenge: **players prefer certain roles**, so matchmaking must fill *both* sides:
- If everyone wants to be the killer (or nobody does), queues break. Design and incentives must keep *both* roles appealing and populated.
- Role-queue, incentives for the less-popular role, and balancing fun across sides are practical necessities, not afterthoughts.

Design asymmetric multiplayer around **genuinely different, non-mirrored roles**, balanced for **comparable win-rates and fun** (not symmetry) across all skill levels, often driven by **information asymmetry** and mind-games, ensuring a **satisfying power fantasy on every side** with **counterplay and tension** — while keeping **all roles popular enough** to matchmake. The magic is two different games colliding into one thrilling contest; the difficulty is keeping that collision fair and fun for everyone.
