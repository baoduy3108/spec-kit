# Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)

Jobs-to-Be-Done is a framework for understanding **why customers actually buy and use products**. Its core reframe: people don't buy products for their features or because of their demographics — they **"hire" a product to make progress on a "job"** they're trying to get done in their life. Focusing on the underlying **job** (and the outcome they want) rather than the product or the persona leads to better, less-obvious product decisions (see product-discovery, jobs... this, north-star-metric).

## The Core Idea: Customers Hire Products to Do a Job

The mental model: a customer has a **job** (a goal, a problem, a desired progress) and **"hires"** a product to accomplish it — and will **"fire"** it for something better. So the question isn't "what features does the user want?" or "what's this user's age/segment?" but **"what job are they hiring this for?"** The famous example (Christensen): a fast-food chain studied who buys **milkshakes** in the morning — and found people "hired" the milkshake for the job of *making a boring commute more interesting and staving off hunger till lunch*. Its competitors weren't other milkshakes — they were bananas and bagels. That insight (improve the milkshake **for that job**) is invisible if you only look at demographics or features.

## The Three Dimensions of a Job

A job isn't just functional; it has layers:
- **Functional** — the practical task ("get to work with breakfast handled").
- **Emotional** — how they want to **feel** ("not bored", "confident", "in control").
- **Social** — how they want to be **perceived** by others.
Great products serve all three. Ignoring the emotional/social dimensions misses why people really choose one option over another.

## Why It's Powerful

- **Focuses on the durable need, not the solution** — jobs are **stable** over time even as technologies/products change (people always needed to "get from A to B"; the solutions evolved). Building around the job future-proofs your thinking.
- **Reveals real competition** — your competitors are **anything** customers hire for the same job (the milkshake vs a banana), not just similar products.
- **Uncovers non-obvious opportunities** — understanding the job surfaces unmet outcomes features-thinking misses.
- **Guides the "why"** — pairs with outcome-based discovery (see opportunity-solution-tree) and North Star metrics (see north-star-metric).

## Job Stories vs User Stories

A practical output: **job stories** frame requirements around the job/context rather than a persona:
- User story: *"As a [persona], I want [feature] so that [benefit]."*
- **Job story**: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."*
Job stories emphasize the **triggering situation and desired outcome**, keeping the team focused on the job, not a made-up persona.

## Design Guidance

- **Ask "what job are they hiring this for?"** — not "what feature do they want" or "who is the demographic".
- **Interview for the job** — the situation, the struggle, what they "hired" before, why they'd switch (JTBD interviews probe the moment of decision).
- **Cover functional + emotional + social** dimensions.
- **Define the desired outcome/progress**, not the feature — build to that outcome.
- **Map real competition** as everything hired for the same job.
- **Use job stories** to keep requirements outcome- and situation-focused.

## Pitfalls (in understanding/using)

- Confusing the **job** with the **product/feature** → you optimize the solution, not the underlying need.
- Defining customers by **demographics** instead of the job → miss the real motivation (the milkshake insight).
- Ignoring **emotional/social** dimensions → miss why people actually choose.
- Framing competition as only **similar products** → blindsided by unexpected alternatives.
- Building **features** without knowing the **outcome** the job requires.
- Treating JTBD as a rigid template rather than a **lens** for uncovering motivation.
