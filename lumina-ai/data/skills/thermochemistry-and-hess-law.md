# Thermochemistry & Hess's Law (Advanced Chemistry)

Thermochemistry studies the **energy changes** in chemical reactions — the heat absorbed or released, and ultimately *why* reactions happen. It's a quantitative advanced/gifted-exam (chuyên) topic linking bond energies, enthalpy, and (at the deepest level) entropy and free energy.

## Enthalpy & Heat of Reaction

- **Enthalpy (H)** — heat content at constant pressure. **ΔH** = enthalpy change = heat absorbed/released by a reaction.
- **Exothermic** (ΔH < 0) — releases heat (combustion, most spontaneous reactions); products lower in energy.
- **Endothermic** (ΔH > 0) — absorbs heat (photosynthesis, melting); products higher in energy.
- Comes from **breaking bonds** (costs energy) vs **forming bonds** (releases energy) — the net is ΔH (see chemical-bonding).

## Calorimetry

- **Measuring heat** — q = mcΔT (mass × specific heat × temperature change) for a substance absorbing heat; a **calorimeter** measures reaction heat by the temperature change of surrounding water.
- Links measurable temperature changes to ΔH. A common experimental problem type.

## Hess's Law (The Key Tool)

- **Hess's law** — ΔH for a reaction is the **same regardless of the pathway** (because enthalpy is a **state function** — depends only on start and end states).
- **Consequence** — you can add/subtract known reactions (and their ΔH values) to find the ΔH of a target reaction you can't measure directly. Reverse a reaction → flip the sign of ΔH; scale a reaction → scale ΔH.
- A powerful calculation method: build the target from known steps.

## Standard Enthalpies of Formation

- **ΔH°f** — the enthalpy change to form 1 mole of a compound from its elements in standard states (elements have ΔH°f = 0).
- **ΔH°reaction = Σ ΔH°f(products) − Σ ΔH°f(reactants)** — compute any reaction's enthalpy from tabulated formation values (a direct application of Hess's law).

## Bond Energies

- **Bond energy** — energy to break one mole of a bond. **ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)** — estimate reaction enthalpy from bond energies. Breaking is endothermic, forming exothermic.

## Entropy & Gibbs Free Energy (Spontaneity)

The deepest question — *will* a reaction happen?
- **Entropy (S)** — a measure of disorder/dispersal; the second law says the universe's entropy increases (see thermodynamics-and-entropy). **ΔS** — the reaction's entropy change (gases, more particles, dissolving → higher entropy).
- **Gibbs free energy** — **ΔG = ΔH − TΔS** — determines **spontaneity**:
  - **ΔG < 0** → spontaneous (favorable); **ΔG > 0** → non-spontaneous; **ΔG = 0** → equilibrium.
- **Temperature dependence** — an endothermic reaction (ΔH > 0) can become spontaneous at high T if ΔS > 0 (the −TΔS term dominates). This explains why enthalpy alone doesn't decide spontaneity — both energy *and* disorder matter.
- ΔG relates to the equilibrium constant K (ΔG° = −RT ln K — see chemical-equilibrium-and-kinetics).

Master thermochemistry via **enthalpy** (ΔH, exo/endothermic from bond making/breaking), **calorimetry** (q = mcΔT), **Hess's law** (path-independence — add known reactions to find ΔH), **standard enthalpies of formation** (ΔH°rxn = ΣΔH°f products − reactants), **bond energies**, and — for spontaneity — **entropy and Gibbs free energy** (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS: negative means spontaneous, with temperature tipping the balance). Understanding that both energy (ΔH) and disorder (ΔS) govern whether reactions occur is the conceptual depth gifted-exam chemistry rewards.
