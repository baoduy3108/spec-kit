# Contract Law Fundamentals

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement. Contract law governs when a promise binds, what the terms mean, and what happens when someone breaks it. The principles below are broadly shared across common-law systems, but **specifics vary by jurisdiction** — this is a general educational overview, **not legal advice**; consult a qualified lawyer for real matters.

## Formation: When Is There a Contract?

A valid contract generally requires several elements:
- **Offer** — a clear proposal to be bound on specific terms.
- **Acceptance** — unqualified agreement to that offer (a change in terms is a *counter-offer*, not acceptance). "Meeting of the minds."
- **Consideration** — each side gives something of value (money, goods, a promise, forbearance). A bare one-sided promise usually isn't enforceable (in common law) without it.
- **Intention to create legal relations** — parties must intend to be legally bound (commercial deals presumed yes; casual social promises presumed no).
- **Capacity** — parties must be legally able to contract (age, mental capacity).
- **Legality** — the purpose must be lawful.

Contracts can be **written or oral** (though some types — e.g. real estate, guarantees — must be in writing under "statute of frauds" rules). Written contracts are strongly preferable for proof and clarity.

## Terms & Interpretation

- **Express terms** — what the parties actually stated.
- **Implied terms** — read in by law, custom, or to give the contract business efficacy.
- **Conditions vs warranties** — a *condition* is a fundamental term (breach lets the other party terminate + claim damages); a *warranty* is minor (damages only).
- **Interpretation** — courts read terms objectively (what a reasonable person would understand), giving words their ordinary meaning in context. Ambiguity may be construed against the drafter (*contra proferentem*).

Clarity in drafting prevents disputes — vague terms are litigation fuel.

## Breach & Remedies

A **breach** is failing to perform an obligation. Remedies aim to address the harm:
- **Damages** — money to put the innocent party in the position they'd have been in had the contract been performed (**expectation** measure). Must be foreseeable and mitigated; penalty clauses are generally unenforceable (genuine pre-estimates of loss — "liquidated damages" — are OK).
- **Specific performance** — a court order to actually perform (rare; for unique goods/property where money won't do).
- **Termination/rescission** — ending the contract (for serious breach).
- **Injunction** — ordering someone to stop doing something.

## Common Clauses (Know These)

- **Indemnity** — one party agrees to cover certain losses/liabilities of the other.
- **Limitation/exclusion of liability** — caps or excludes liability (subject to legal limits; can't usually exclude liability for fraud/death).
- **Termination** — how/when parties can end the contract (for cause, for convenience, notice periods).
- **Warranties & representations** — assurances about facts; breach has different consequences.
- **Force majeure** — excuses performance for extraordinary events (disasters, war).
- **Confidentiality, governing law & jurisdiction, dispute resolution (arbitration), assignment, entire agreement.**

These clauses allocate **risk** — the heart of commercial contracting.

## Void, Voidable & Unenforceable

- **Void** — no contract at all (e.g. illegal purpose).
- **Voidable** — valid but one party can cancel (e.g. induced by misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, or lacking capacity).
- **Unenforceable** — valid but a court won't enforce (e.g. required writing missing).

Contract law is about **when a promise binds (formation), what it means (terms), and what happens if broken (breach and remedies)** — with well-drafted clauses allocating risk clearly. The practical lesson: put it in writing, be precise, and (for anything significant) get a lawyer — because jurisdiction and detail decide real outcomes.
