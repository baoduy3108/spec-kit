# The Twelve Principles of Animation

Codified by Disney animators (Thomas & Johnston, *The Illusion of Life*), the twelve principles are the foundation of believable motion — for hand-drawn film, 3D, games, and UI alike. They're not about realism; they're about making movement feel **alive, weighty, and readable**.

## Weight & Physicality

- **Squash & stretch** — objects deform to convey mass and speed (a bouncing ball flattens on impact, stretches in flight) while keeping constant volume. The single most important principle for making things feel physical rather than rigid.
- **Slow in & slow out (easing)** — motion accelerates and decelerates; nothing starts or stops instantly. Clustering frames at the ends of a move is what makes it feel natural (see easing-and-animation-timing).
- **Arcs** — living things move along curved paths, not straight lines. A hand, a head turn, a thrown ball all arc. Straight-line motion reads as robotic.

## Readability & Intent

- **Anticipation** — a wind-up before an action (crouch before a jump, pull back before a punch) prepares the viewer and gives the action power. Without it, actions feel abrupt and get missed.
- **Staging** — present the idea clearly: pose, framing, and timing direct the eye to what matters, one clear idea at a time. Silhouette readability is key.
- **Exaggeration** — push poses and timing beyond literal reality to communicate energy and emotion. Restrained-but-heightened, not cartoonish for its own sake.

## Life & Nuance

- **Follow-through & overlapping action** — parts keep moving after the main body stops (hair, cloth, tails settle late), and different parts move on slightly different timings. This "drag" is what removes the stiff, everything-stops-at-once look.
- **Secondary action** — supporting motion that enriches the main one (a character walks *and* swings their arms / hums), adding life without distracting.
- **Timing** — the number of frames a move takes defines weight and mood; few frames = fast/light, many = slow/heavy. Timing *is* the performance.

## Craft

- **Solid drawing** — respect form, volume, weight, and balance in 3D space (in CG: good rigging and posing) so figures feel dimensional, not flat.
- **Appeal** — charisma and clarity of design; a character (or motion) that's pleasing and easy to read. Appeal isn't "cute" — it's well-designed and engaging.
- **Straight-ahead vs pose-to-pose** — two workflows: draw frame-by-frame in sequence (fluid, spontaneous — good for fire, action) vs. set key poses first then fill breakdowns/in-betweens (controlled, planned — most character work). Often combined.

## Applying to UI & Games

These aren't just for cartoons. **UI micro-interactions** use anticipation (a button dips before it acts), squash & stretch (a bouncy modal), easing (everything), follow-through (a list settling), and staging (motion guiding attention). **Game feel** leans on squash & stretch, anticipation, exaggeration, and timing for juice (see game-feel-and-juice). Motion that ignores these reads as cheap; motion that honors them feels crafted and alive.

The through-line: real things have weight, prepare before they act, move in arcs, and settle gradually. Encode that, and even abstract shapes feel believable.
