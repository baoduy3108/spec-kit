# Macroeconomics Fundamentals

Macroeconomics studies the **economy as a whole** — total output, employment, inflation, and growth — and how governments and central banks influence them. Where microeconomics zooms into individual markets, macro zooms out to national and global aggregates and the policy levers that steer them.

## GDP & Economic Growth

- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** — the total value of goods/services produced in an economy over a period; the headline measure of size and activity. **Real GDP** adjusts for inflation (vs nominal). GDP = C + I + G + NX (consumption + investment + government + net exports).
- **Growth** — the rise in real GDP over time; long-run growth (driven by productivity, capital, labor, technology) is what raises living standards. Small percentage differences compound enormously over decades.
- GDP has limits (ignores inequality, non-market work, wellbeing, environment) but remains the core aggregate.

## Inflation

- **Inflation** — a general rise in prices (fall in money's purchasing power), measured by indices like CPI. Moderate inflation (~2%) is typically targeted; **deflation** (falling prices) and **hyperinflation** are both dangerous.
- **Causes** — demand-pull (too much demand), cost-push (rising costs), and monetary (too much money chasing goods).
- Inflation erodes savings, redistributes between debtors/creditors, and distorts decisions — controlling it is a central-bank priority.

## Unemployment & the Business Cycle

- **Unemployment** — the share of the labor force wanting work but jobless. Types: frictional (between jobs), structural (skills/location mismatch), cyclical (from downturns). "Full employment" allows some natural unemployment.
- **The business cycle** — economies fluctuate through **expansion → peak → contraction (recession) → trough → recovery**. Recessions bring falling output and rising unemployment; policy aims to smooth these swings.
- **Okun's law** and the **Phillips curve** (an inflation–unemployment tradeoff, at least short-run) link these aggregates.

## Monetary Policy

Run by the **central bank** (Fed, ECB, SBV), monetary policy manages **money supply and interest rates**:
- **Interest rates** — the primary lever. Lowering rates (expansionary) stimulates borrowing, spending, and investment (fights recession); raising rates (contractionary) cools an overheating economy and fights inflation.
- **Tools** — the policy rate, open-market operations, reserve requirements, and (modern) quantitative easing.
- **Dual mandate** — typically balancing stable inflation and maximum employment. Central-bank credibility and expectations matter enormously.

## Fiscal Policy

Run by the **government**, fiscal policy uses **spending and taxation**:
- **Expansionary** — more spending / lower taxes to boost demand in downturns (often via deficits).
- **Contractionary** — less spending / higher taxes to cool inflation or reduce debt.
- **Multiplier effect** — a dollar of spending can ripple into more than a dollar of activity.
- **Deficits and debt** — persistent gaps between spending and revenue accumulate as national debt, with long-run sustainability concerns.

## How They Interact

The economy is a system: monetary and fiscal policy interact (and can reinforce or offset each other); inflation, unemployment, and growth trade off and feed back; expectations shape outcomes; and open economies are linked by trade and exchange rates. Policy is about **stabilizing** the cycle (avoiding deep recessions and runaway inflation) while fostering long-run growth — amid uncertainty, lags, and debate between schools (Keynesian, monetarist, etc.).

Reason about the whole economy using **GDP/growth** (output and living standards), **inflation** (price stability), **unemployment and the business cycle** (fluctuations), **monetary policy** (central banks, interest rates), and **fiscal policy** (government spending/taxes) — understanding how these aggregates interact and how policy tries to stabilize the cycle while promoting durable growth.
