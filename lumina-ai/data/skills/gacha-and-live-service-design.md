# Gacha & Live-Service Design

Gacha games ("gacha" = capsule-toy machine) are live-service, free-to-play games built around **randomized pulls** for collectible characters/items, monetized by selling the currency to pull. They're wildly lucrative and deeply controversial. Designing one means engineering compelling collection and retention loops — and it demands genuine **ethical care**, because the same mechanics that engage can exploit.

## The Gacha Pull

The core mechanic: spend currency to **pull** from a randomized pool for characters/gear of varying **rarity** (common → ultra-rare):
- **Variable-ratio rewards** — the unpredictable payoff (the same psychology as slot machines) is intensely compelling; the *anticipation* of a rare pull is the hook.
- **Rarity tiers** with low odds for the best units create desire and chase.
- The pull *moment* is dressed with hype (animations, effects) to maximize the dopamine of a rare hit.

This is powerful and, precisely because it's powerful, the ethical fault line of the genre.

## Collection & Progression

Beyond the pull, the game loop is **collect and grow**:
- **Collection** — assembling a roster of characters, each with art, story, and mechanics, drives long-term engagement (gotta collect 'em all).
- **Progression** — leveling, ascending, gearing, and duplicating units gives depth and long-term goals; even after pulling a unit, there's a long investment path.
- Gameplay (turn-based, action, puzzle, etc.) sits on top — the gacha is the *acquisition* layer, and a good game needs the actual gameplay to be fun, not just the pulls.

## Currency Design

Multiple currencies structure the economy and monetization:
- **Soft currency** — earned freely through play (for upgrades).
- **Hard/premium currency** — the pull currency, earned slowly *or bought with money*. The exchange between play-time and money runs through this.
- **Energy/stamina** — limits play sessions, pacing engagement (and sometimes monetized to bypass).

The design of earn rates vs costs (the "grind") and how premium currency is doled out defines the free-vs-paid experience.

## Pity Systems

To soften brutal RNG, **pity** guarantees a rare after N pulls (a "hard pity" ceiling; sometimes "soft pity" ramps odds up as you approach). Pity:
- Makes the chase feel *fair-ish* — you *will* get the featured unit eventually, bounding worst-case bad luck.
- Is itself a monetization/psychology tool (players spend to reach pity). A more *humane* pity (transparent, reasonable) is a key ethical lever.

## Retention Loops

Live-service lives on **daily engagement**:
- **Daily quests, login bonuses, energy refills, events** — reasons to return every day; streaks and expiring rewards drive habit.
- **Limited-time banners/events** — new characters available for a window, creating **FOMO** (fear of missing out) that pressures pulling/spending *now*. This is highly effective and highly manipulable.
- **Content cadence** — a constant stream of new units, events, and story keeps the game alive (a treadmill for developers, like MMOs).

## Monetization & Whales

- Revenue is heavily **top-heavy** — a small fraction of "**whales**" (big spenders) drive most income, while most players spend little/nothing (free players provide population, competition, and community).
- Design tension: serve free players well enough to retain them *and* offer whales things to spend on — without making it pay-to-win enough to alienate everyone else.

## Ethics (Not Optional)

These mechanics border on gambling and can genuinely **exploit** — especially minors and people with addictive tendencies. Responsible design:
- **Transparency** — publish real drop rates (legally required in many regions), clear pity, no deceptive odds.
- **Guardrails** — spending limits, age gates, no predatory dark patterns (see notification/dark-pattern awareness), avoid designing for compulsion over fun.
- **Fair free path** — playable and generous enough that money is a convenience, not a wall.
- **Regulatory awareness** — loot boxes are banned/restricted in some countries; know the law.

The honest stance: gacha *can* be a fun collection game done fairly, or a manipulation engine. Design for the player's genuine enjoyment and well-being, not just maximized spend.

Design gacha/live-service around a **compelling pull-and-collect loop**, layered **currencies** and **pity** that make RNG feel fair, **daily and event retention loops**, and sustainable **monetization** — while treating **ethics as a core requirement**: transparency, guardrails, a fair free experience, and a refusal to exploit. The best of the genre respects players; the worst preys on them — and that choice is the designer's.
