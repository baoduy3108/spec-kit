# Writing Well in Russian

Russian is a highly inflected Slavic language written in **Cyrillic**. Its defining challenges are a **six-case system** that shapes almost every noun/adjective, and **verb aspect** (perfective vs imperfective) that pervades the verb system. It has **no articles** and relatively **flexible word order** thanks to case marking. Writing well means getting the inflections and aspect right while achieving natural Russian flow.

## Cyrillic Alphabet

Russian uses the **Cyrillic** script (33 letters). Some letters resemble Latin ones but differ (В=v, Н=n, Р=r, С=s, У=u, Х=kh), plus unique letters (ж ц ч ш щ ю я ъ ь). Clean, correct Cyrillic (including the soft/hard signs ь/ъ, which affect pronunciation and meaning) is basic literacy; stress (unmarked in normal text but affecting vowel reduction) is learned per word.

## The Six Cases (The Core Challenge)

Russian nouns, adjectives, pronouns, and numerals decline through **six cases**, each marking a grammatical role:
- **Nominative** (subject), **Accusative** (direct object), **Genitive** (possession/"of"/absence), **Dative** (indirect object/"to"), **Instrumental** ("by/with"), **Prepositional** (location/"about").
- Cases change word **endings** based on case + gender + number, and **prepositions govern specific cases** (в + prepositional for location, в + accusative for direction). Adjectives agree with their nouns in case, gender, and number.

This case system is the heart of Russian grammar and the main hurdle — endings must be correct, and there's a lot to internalize (declension tables). Correct case usage is what makes Russian right.

## Three Genders

Nouns are **masculine, feminine, or neuter** (largely predictable from endings: consonant → masc., -а/-я → fem., -о/-е → neut., with exceptions). Gender drives adjective and past-tense verb agreement. Basic and pervasive.

## Verb Aspect (Perfective/Imperfective)

Russian's other big challenge: nearly every verb comes in an **aspect pair**:
- **Imperfective** — ongoing, repeated, habitual, or process actions ("I was reading," "I read regularly").
- **Perfective** — completed, single, result-focused actions ("I read [and finished] it").

Choosing the right aspect is essential and subtle — it's *not* the same as tense (Russian has fewer tenses but this aspect distinction instead). Aspect pervades all verb usage and is one of the hardest things for learners to master.

## No Articles; Flexible Word Order

- **No articles** — Russian has no "a"/"the"; definiteness is conveyed by context, word order, and other means. (Native English speakers over-add; Russian speakers writing English under-add.)
- **Flexible word order** — because cases mark grammatical roles, word order is freer than English and used for **emphasis and information flow** (new/emphasized info often at the end). SVO is neutral, but rearranging shifts nuance rather than breaking grammar. Using word order well for emphasis is a fluency marker.

## Formality & Natural Phrasing

- **ты (ty) vs вы (vy)** — informal singular ты (friends, family, peers) vs formal/plural вы (strangers, elders, respect, and all plurals). Choose by relationship; вы when unsure/respectful.
- **Register** — formal Russian is precise and can be quite formal in official contexts; casual Russian uses colloquialisms, diminutives (a rich, affectionate system — Ivan → Vanya → Vanechka), and particles.
- **Natural phrasing** — Russian idiom, aspect, and case-driven structure differ from English; avoid literal translation. Diminutives and particles (же, ведь, -то, ну) add tone.

Write Russian well by producing correct **Cyrillic**, above all mastering the **six-case declension** (endings by case/gender/number, preposition-governed cases, adjective agreement) and **verb aspect** (perfective vs imperfective — the two core challenges), handling **three genders**, working **without articles**, using **flexible word order** for emphasis, and choosing **ты/вы** appropriately with natural idiom and diminutives. Good Russian is correctly-declined, aspect-accurate, and flows in its own case-driven, article-free logic.
