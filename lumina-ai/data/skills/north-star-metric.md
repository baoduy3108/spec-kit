# The North Star Metric

A North Star Metric (NSM) is the **single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to its customers** — the one number that, if it goes up, means customers are getting more of what they came for, and the business will grow as a result. Its purpose is **alignment**: it gives every team one shared, meaningful goal, so efforts point the same direction instead of optimizing conflicting local metrics (see goal-setting-okrs, opportunity-solution-tree, saas-metrics).

## The Idea: One Metric That Means "Customers Getting Value"

Products have dozens of metrics, and teams can pull in different directions (marketing chases signups, engineering chases uptime, sales chases revenue) — sometimes at customers' expense. The North Star cuts through this: pick **one** metric that represents **delivered customer value**, and rally the whole company around growing it. Classic examples: Spotify's *time spent listening*, Airbnb's *nights booked*, WhatsApp's *messages sent*, a collaboration tool's *weekly active teams*. Each captures the moment customers **actually get value** from the product.

## What Makes a Good North Star

- **Reflects customer value** — it goes up when customers get the core benefit (not just when you extract money). If customers win, the metric rises.
- **Leading, not lagging** — it should **predict** long-term growth/revenue, so improving it today drives results tomorrow. (Revenue itself is a **lagging** result, not a good North Star.)
- **Actionable** — teams can actually influence it through their work.
- **Simple and shared** — one metric everyone understands and can rally behind.
The test: *"if this number grows, are our customers genuinely better off and is the business healthier?"*

## Why Not Revenue or Vanity Metrics?

- **Revenue** is a **lagging** outcome and can grow while customers are underserved (aggressive monetization, churn masked by new sales) — it doesn't guide *value* creation. Revenue is the *result* of delivering value, not the North Star for delivering it.
- **Vanity metrics** (total signups, page views, downloads) look good but don't reflect **value delivered or retention** — you can inflate them without customers succeeding. A North Star should be **value/engagement-based**, not a vanity count.

## Input Metrics: The Levers

The North Star is often too high-level to act on directly, so you break it into **input metrics** — the **drivers** teams can move that add up to the North Star. Example: if NSM = "weekly active teams", inputs might be *new teams activated*, *teams retained*, *features adopted per team*. Teams own inputs; the sum moves the North Star. This connects daily work to the guiding metric (and ladders up from opportunities in an OST — see opportunity-solution-tree).

## Design Guidance

- **Pick one metric that captures core customer value** — the "aha"/value moment, not money or vanity counts.
- **Make it leading** — it should predict growth, not just record it.
- **Decompose into input metrics** teams can directly influence.
- **Align everyone** on it — the point is shared direction.
- **Balance with guardrails** — watch that optimizing the NSM doesn't harm other things (quality, churn); pair with counter-metrics.
- **Revisit** as the product/strategy evolves — the right North Star can change.

## Pitfalls (in understanding/using)

- Choosing **revenue** as the North Star → it's lagging and can rise while customers are underserved; it guides extraction, not value.
- **Vanity metrics** (signups, views) → inflatable without real value/retention.
- A North Star that's **too high-level** with no **input metrics** → teams can't act on it.
- **Gaming** the metric (Goodhart's law) → optimize the number while harming customers; use guardrail counter-metrics.
- **Multiple** "north stars" → defeats the alignment purpose; pick one.
- Never **revisiting** it as strategy changes → optimizing a stale target.
