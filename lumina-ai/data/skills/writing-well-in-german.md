# Writing Well in German

German is an inflected Germanic language with a reputation for **precision and structural rigor**. Writing well means mastering its **case system**, three genders, distinctive **word order** (verbs in specific positions), famous **compound nouns**, and the rule that **all nouns are capitalized** — plus the du/Sie formality choice.

## The Four Cases & Three Genders

German nouns have **gender** (masculine der, feminine die, neuter das — often unpredictable) and decline through **four cases**:
- **Nominative** (subject), **Accusative** (direct object), **Dative** (indirect object), **Genitive** (possession).
- Cases change articles, adjective endings, and some pronouns (der/den/dem/des). Prepositions govern specific cases (mit + dative, für + accusative, some "two-way"). Adjective endings depend on gender + case + article type — a notorious table to master.

Correct case and gender agreement is the core grammatical challenge and a strong literacy marker. Learn each noun *with its gender and plural*.

## Du vs Sie (Formality)

German has informal **du** (friends, family, children, peers) and formal **Sie** (strangers, professional, respect — capitalized, takes plural verb form). Choosing wrong matters (du to a stranger/superior is over-familiar). Default to **Sie** in professional/unfamiliar contexts until invited to du. This T-V distinction is a real social marker.

## Word Order: V2 and Verb-Final

German word order is distinctive and strict in its own way:
- **V2 (verb-second)** in main clauses — the finite verb is always the *second element* (whatever comes first, the verb follows): "Heute **gehe** ich…" ("Today go I…").
- **Verb-final in subordinate clauses** — after conjunctions like weil, dass, wenn, the finite verb goes to the **end**: "…weil ich müde **bin**" ("…because I tired am").
- **Separable-verb parts and infinitives/participles** go to the end of main clauses ("Ich **rufe** dich morgen **an**" — anrufen split; "Ich habe es **gemacht**").

This "verb at the end" tendency (especially the long build-up in complex sentences) is a hallmark of German and a common learner hurdle.

## Compound Nouns

German freely **combines words into long compound nouns** — Handschuh (hand-shoe = glove), Geschwindigkeitsbegrenzung (speed limit), and the famous long bureaucratic monsters. Compounds are written as *one word*, and their gender comes from the last component. This productivity lets German name precise concepts compactly; using and parsing compounds is part of fluency.

## Noun Capitalization

A distinctive rule: **all nouns are capitalized** in German (das Haus, die Freiheit), not just proper nouns. This is mandatory and pervasive — getting it right (and not over-capitalizing other words) is basic German literacy.

## Separable Verbs & Register

- **Separable verbs** — many verbs have prefixes that detach and move to the clause's end in main clauses (ankommen → "Ich **komme** um acht **an**"). Tracking the split-off prefix is essential.
- **Register** — formal German (business, official) is precise, uses Sie, the genitive, and full constructions; casual German uses du, colloquialisms, and simpler structures. Formal writing conventions (letter forms) are fairly rigid.
- **Modal particles** (doch, mal, ja, halt, eben) add nuance/tone that's hard to translate but makes speech natural.

Write German well by mastering **gender and the four-case declension** (articles, adjective endings, prepositional cases), choosing **du vs Sie** appropriately, obeying **V2 and verb-final word order** (verbs in their required positions, separable prefixes to the end), using **compound nouns** naturally, **capitalizing all nouns**, and matching **register** with natural modal-particle nuance. Good German is precisely-declined, correctly-ordered, and structurally exact — the language rewards rigor.
