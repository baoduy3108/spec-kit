# Pharmacology & Drug Action

Pharmacology is the science of how drugs affect the body and how the body handles drugs. Two complementary questions frame it: **what the body does to the drug** (pharmacokinetics) and **what the drug does to the body** (pharmacodynamics). *(Educational overview; not medical advice — dosing and drug decisions require professional judgment.)*

## Pharmacokinetics (ADME): What the Body Does to the Drug

The journey of a drug through the body, governing its concentration over time:
- **Absorption** — how the drug enters the bloodstream (oral, IV, etc.). **Bioavailability** = the fraction reaching circulation (IV = 100%; oral is reduced by gut absorption and first-pass liver metabolism).
- **Distribution** — how it spreads to tissues (blood flow, protein binding, lipid solubility, barriers like the blood-brain barrier). **Volume of distribution** describes how widely it disperses.
- **Metabolism** — chemical transformation, mostly in the **liver** (cytochrome P450 enzymes), usually to more water-soluble, often inactive forms (sometimes activating "prodrugs").
- **Excretion** — elimination, mainly via **kidneys** (urine) or bile.

ADME determines dose, route, and frequency. Impaired liver/kidney function alters metabolism/excretion — a key reason doses are individualized.

## Half-Life & Dosing

**Half-life (t½)** — the time for drug concentration to halve. It sets:
- **Dosing frequency** — short t½ needs frequent dosing; long t½ allows once-daily.
- **Time to steady state** — ~4–5 half-lives to reach stable levels with repeated dosing (and to clear after stopping).
- **Loading vs maintenance dose** — a larger initial dose reaches therapeutic levels fast when t½ is long.

The goal is keeping concentration in the **therapeutic window** — above the minimum effective level, below the toxic level.

## Pharmacodynamics: What the Drug Does to the Body

How drugs produce effects, usually by binding molecular targets (mostly **receptors**, also enzymes, ion channels, transporters):
- **Agonist** — binds and *activates* a receptor (mimics the natural signal).
- **Antagonist** — binds and *blocks* it (prevents activation), competitively or not.
- **Partial agonist** — activates submaximally.
- **Affinity** (how tightly it binds) and **efficacy** (how much effect once bound) characterize the interaction.

**Dose-response** — effect increases with dose up to a maximum (**Emax**); **potency** (the EC50 — dose for half-max effect) lets drugs be compared. Selectivity for the intended target vs others determines side effects.

## Therapeutic Index & Safety

The **therapeutic index (TI)** = ratio of toxic dose to effective dose. A **wide TI** (e.g. many common drugs) is forgiving; a **narrow TI** (e.g. warfarin, digoxin, lithium) means small dosing errors risk toxicity or failure — these require monitoring. TI is the core measure of a drug's safety margin.

## Interactions & Adaptation

- **Drug interactions** — one drug alters another's kinetics (e.g. inhibiting/inducing P450 enzymes changes metabolism) or dynamics (additive/opposing effects). A major source of adverse events, especially with polypharmacy.
- **Tolerance** — reduced effect with repeated use (receptor downregulation), needing higher doses.
- **Dependence & withdrawal**, **tachyphylaxis** (rapid tolerance), and **genetic variation** (pharmacogenomics — why people respond differently) further individualize response.

Pharmacology is understanding a drug through **ADME (concentration over time) × pharmacodynamics (target binding → effect)**, kept within a therapeutic window sized by half-life and therapeutic index — while accounting for interactions, tolerance, and individual variation that make dosing a personalized science.
