# Organic Chemistry Basics (THPT Chemistry)

Organic chemistry is the study of **carbon compounds** — the chemistry of life, fuels, plastics, and medicines. It's a large, heavily-weighted part of the THPT chemistry exam. What makes it a whole field is **carbon's unique ability to bond** in endless chains, rings, and structures.

## Why Carbon?

Carbon is special because it:
- Forms **four covalent bonds** (see chemical-bonding), letting it build vast, stable structures.
- **Catenates** — bonds to *itself* in long chains and rings.
- Bonds to H, O, N, S, halogens, creating enormous diversity.

This is why millions of organic compounds exist from just a few elements. The **carbon skeleton** (chain/ring) plus attached groups defines each molecule.

## Hydrocarbons

Compounds of only **carbon and hydrogen**, the simplest organics:
- **Alkanes** (ankan) — only single C–C bonds, "saturated" (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: methane CH₄, ethane, propane…). Relatively unreactive; fuels. Undergo **substitution** and **combustion**.
- **Alkenes** (anken) — contain a C=C **double bond**, "unsaturated" (CₙH₂ₙ). More reactive; undergo **addition** reactions across the double bond.
- **Alkynes** (ankin) — contain a C≡C **triple bond** (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂). Even more reactive.
- **Aromatics** — benzene ring (C₆H₆) with special stability from delocalized electrons; undergo substitution.

The degree of saturation (single vs multiple bonds) drives reactivity.

## Functional Groups

The reactive part of a molecule that determines its properties and chemistry — the key organizing idea:
- **Alcohol** (–OH, ancol), **aldehyde** (–CHO, anđehit), **ketone** (C=O), **carboxylic acid** (–COOH, axit — acidic), **ester** (–COO–, esters — fragrant, from acid + alcohol), **ether**, **amine** (–NH₂, amin — basic), **amide**, **halide**.
- Molecules with the **same functional group** behave similarly. Learning the groups lets you predict a compound's properties and reactions — the heart of organic chemistry (and the exam).

## Isomerism

**Isomers** — molecules with the **same molecular formula but different structures** (and thus different properties):
- **Structural isomers** — different connectivity (chain, position, functional-group isomers).
- **Stereoisomers** — same connectivity, different 3D arrangement (cis/trans around double bonds; optical isomers/chirality).
Isomerism explains why one formula can be many distinct substances — a common THPT problem type (counting isomers).

## Naming (Nomenclature)

**IUPAC naming** systematically encodes structure: a **root** (chain length: meth-1, eth-2, prop-3, but-4, pent-5…), a **suffix** for the functional group (-ane, -ene, -ol, -oic acid…), and **prefixes/numbers** for substituents and positions. Naming lets you translate between name and structure — a basic required skill.

## Key Reaction Types

- **Substitution** — an atom/group replaces another (alkanes, aromatics).
- **Addition** — atoms add across a double/triple bond (alkenes, alkynes — e.g. hydrogenation, hydration).
- **Elimination** — removing atoms to form a double bond (reverse of addition).
- **Esterification** — acid + alcohol → ester + water (and hydrolysis, the reverse).
- **Oxidation/reduction, polymerization** (small monomers → plastics), **combustion**.
Recognizing the reaction type from the functional groups involved is central exam skill.

Master THPT organic chemistry via **carbon's four-bond versatility** (chains, rings), the **hydrocarbon families** (alkane/alkene/alkyne/aromatic and their saturation-driven reactivity), **functional groups** (the reactive centers that define behavior — the key organizing principle), **isomerism** (same formula, different structure), **IUPAC naming**, and the **reaction types** (substitution, addition, esterification…). Focus on functional groups: identify them and you can predict properties and reactions across the whole subject.
