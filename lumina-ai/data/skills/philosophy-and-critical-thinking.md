# Philosophy & Critical Thinking

Philosophy is the disciplined pursuit of answers to life's most fundamental questions — about reality, knowledge, values, and reasoning. Critical thinking is its practical core: the ability to analyze arguments, question assumptions, and reason clearly. Together they underpin rigorous thought in every field.

## The Branches of Philosophy

- **Metaphysics** — the nature of reality and existence: what is real? mind vs matter, free will vs determinism, identity, causation, time.
- **Epistemology** — the nature of knowledge: what can we know, and how? belief, truth, justification, skepticism, the limits of certainty.
- **Ethics** — how we should live and act: what is good, right, just? (below).
- **Logic** — the principles of valid reasoning (see formal-logic-and-argumentation).
- **Aesthetics** (beauty/art) and **political philosophy** (justice, rights, the state).

## Major Ethical Frameworks

Three enduring approaches to "what is the right thing to do?":
- **Consequentialism (Utilitarianism)** — actions are right if they produce the best *outcomes* (the greatest good for the greatest number — Bentham, Mill). Judge by results.
- **Deontology** — actions are right/wrong by *duty and rules*, regardless of consequences (Kant: act only on principles you could will universal; treat people as ends, not means). Judge by the act itself.
- **Virtue ethics** — focus on *character*: what would a virtuous person do? Cultivate good traits (Aristotle: courage, honesty, wisdom). Judge the person.
These frameworks often conflict (the trolley problem pits them against each other) — a rich source of ethical reasoning.

## Key Questions & Thinkers

- Enduring questions: Does God exist? What is consciousness? Do we have free will? What makes a life meaningful? What is justice?
- Major thinkers: **Socrates, Plato, Aristotle** (foundations); **Descartes** (doubt, "I think therefore I am"); **Kant, Hume, Nietzsche, Mill**; Eastern traditions (Confucius, Buddhist philosophy, Laozi).
- Philosophy progresses by *argument* — thinkers building on and challenging each other.

## Critical Thinking (The Practical Skill)

Reasoning clearly and evaluating claims:
- **Analyze arguments** — identify the **premises** (reasons) and **conclusion**; ask whether the premises are *true* and whether they *actually support* the conclusion (validity — see formal-logic).
- **Question assumptions** — surface hidden premises and biases (yours and others' — see cognitive-biases-and-decision-making).
- **Evaluate evidence** — assess source credibility, relevance, sufficiency, and whether correlation is being confused with causation.
- **Consider alternatives** — steelman opposing views; avoid confirmation bias.

## Logical Fallacies

Common **errors in reasoning** to spot and avoid:
- **Ad hominem** (attacking the person, not the argument), **straw man** (misrepresenting a view to refute it), **false dichotomy** (only two options when more exist), **slippery slope**, **appeal to authority/emotion/popularity**, **circular reasoning**, **hasty generalization**, **correlation ≠ causation**.
- Recognizing fallacies protects you from bad arguments and manipulation, and strengthens your own reasoning.

Engage philosophy and critical thinking via the **branches** (metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, logic), the **ethical frameworks** (consequentialist outcomes, deontological duties, virtue-ethics character — and their conflicts), the **big questions and thinkers**, and the practical skills of **analyzing arguments** (premises → conclusion, truth and support), **questioning assumptions**, **evaluating evidence**, and **spotting logical fallacies**. The core discipline — reasoning carefully and questioning deeply rather than accepting claims uncritically — is philosophy's gift to clear thinking in every domain.
