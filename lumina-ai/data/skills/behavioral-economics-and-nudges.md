# Behavioral Economics & Nudges

Behavioral economics fuses psychology with economics to explain how **real people actually decide** — which systematically differs from the perfectly-rational, self-interested "homo economicus" of classical theory. People have limited attention, willpower, and computation, and predictable biases. Understanding these makes for better predictions, designs, and policies.

## Beyond the Rational Agent

Classical economics assumes rational, consistent, self-interested optimizers. Behavioral economics (Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler) shows people are **predictably irrational**:
- **Bounded rationality** — limited information, time, and cognitive capacity; we *satisfice* (good enough) rather than optimize.
- We use **heuristics** (mental shortcuts) that usually work but cause systematic **biases** (see cognitive-biases-and-decision-making).
- Crucially, these deviations are *systematic and predictable* — not random noise — so they can be modeled and designed around.

## Prospect Theory & Loss Aversion

The landmark theory of decision under risk:
- **Loss aversion** — losses hurt roughly **twice as much** as equivalent gains feel good. We fear losing $100 more than we value gaining $100. This drives risk-taking to avoid losses and risk-aversion to protect gains.
- **Reference dependence** — we evaluate outcomes as *changes* from a reference point (the status quo), not absolute states. Framing something as a loss vs a gain flips behavior.
- **Diminishing sensitivity** and **probability weighting** (overweighting rare events — why we buy lottery tickets *and* insurance).

Loss aversion is one of the most powerful, widely-applicable findings — it shapes pricing, framing, and the endowment effect (overvaluing what we own).

## Key Biases in Economic Behavior

- **Mental accounting** — treating money differently by arbitrary "accounts" (splurging a windfall but hoarding salary); money is fungible but we don't treat it so.
- **Present bias / hyperbolic discounting** — over-valuing immediate rewards over future ones, inconsistently (why we procrastinate, under-save, over-eat). We're not exponential discounters.
- **Anchoring, framing, the endowment effect, sunk-cost fallacy, and social proof** all shape economic choices (see cognitive-biases).

## Choice Architecture & Nudges

The design implication: **how choices are presented ("choice architecture") shapes decisions** — so design it deliberately:
- **Defaults** — the pre-selected option is powerful (most people stick with it). Opt-out organ donation or auto-enrollment in retirement savings dramatically raises participation vs opt-in. Defaults are the strongest nudge.
- **A nudge** — a small change in choice architecture that predictably steers behavior *without forbidding options or changing incentives* (Thaler & Sunstein). Examples: placing healthy food at eye level, simplifying forms, timely reminders, framing.
- **Friction and salience** — adding/removing steps, and making the right choice easy and visible, changes behavior.

## Ethics: Nudge vs Manipulation

Nudges are powerful, which makes ethics essential:
- **Libertarian paternalism** — steer toward people's *own* better interests while preserving freedom to choose otherwise. The nudge should help the nudged, be transparent, and keep options open.
- **Dark patterns** (see dark-patterns/gacha ethics) — the same tools used to exploit (manipulative defaults, deceptive framing, engineered addiction) are unethical. The line: does it serve the user or the designer at the user's expense?
- Use behavioral insights to *help* people act on their own goals, transparently — not to manipulate them against their interests.

Apply behavioral economics by recognizing people are **predictably irrational** (bounded rationality, heuristics), leveraging **prospect theory and loss aversion** (losses loom larger, framing/reference points matter), accounting for **mental accounting and present bias**, and designing **choice architecture** — especially **defaults** and well-crafted **nudges** — to steer behavior, always on the **ethical** side of helping people meet their own goals rather than exploiting their biases.
