# "Show, Don't Tell" & Scene Craft

"Show, don't tell" is the most-repeated (and most-misunderstood) writing advice. It means: **let readers experience the story through concrete, sensory scenes rather than being told about it in summary.** Showing creates immersion and emotion; telling conveys information. Mastering *when* to do each — and how to write a vivid scene — is core fiction craft.

## Showing vs Telling

- **Telling** — the narrator states/summarizes: "She was nervous." "The party was fun." "He was a cruel man." Efficient, but distant — the reader is *informed*, not *moved*.
- **Showing** — dramatize it so the reader *infers* it: "Her hands wouldn't stay still; she kept smoothing a dress that didn't need smoothing." Now the reader *experiences* the nervousness and concludes it themselves — which is engaging and emotionally real.

Showing works because readers *participate* — they interpret the evidence, and what they conclude themselves feels true in a way being told never does. It creates immersion, emotion, and trust in the reader's intelligence.

## How to Show: Concrete Sensory Detail

The tools of showing:
- **Sensory, concrete detail** — sight, sound, smell, touch, taste. Specific, tangible images (not abstractions) put the reader *there*. "The room smelled of burnt coffee and old paper" beats "the room was unpleasant."
- **Action and behavior** — reveal character and emotion through what people *do* (see character-development). A clenched jaw, a forced laugh, a slammed door.
- **Body language and physical reaction** — emotions manifest physically (a racing heart, cold hands, a look away). Show the symptom, let the reader name the feeling.
- **Telling detail** — one precise, evocative specific implies more than paragraphs of explanation.

## But Don't Show *Everything*

The crucial nuance beginners miss: **"show don't tell" is not absolute.** Telling has its uses — and showing everything is exhausting and slow:
- **Telling (summary) is right** for transitions, unimportant information, compressing time ("Three years passed."), and pace. You don't dramatize a character brushing their teeth if it doesn't matter.
- **Showing (scene) is right** for **important, emotional, dramatic moments** — the beats you want the reader to *live through*.
- The skill is **choosing what to show**: dramatize what matters, summarize what doesn't. Showing everything buries the important moments in trivia and kills pace.

## Scene vs Summary

This maps to two modes of storytelling:
- **Scene** — real-time dramatization, moment by moment, with sensory detail, action, and dialogue. Immersive, slow, emotional. For key events.
- **Summary (narrative)** — compressed overview covering time/events quickly. For bridging, context, and pace.

Good fiction **alternates** — scenes for the important beats, summary to move efficiently between them. Pacing (see narrative-pacing-and-tension) is largely the rhythm of scene and summary.

## Crafting a Scene

An effective scene:
- **Grounds the reader in place** — establish setting/space with a few sensory details so the action isn't happening in a void ("floating heads talking"). Anchor us physically.
- **Has a goal and conflict/tension** — a scene should *do* something (advance plot, reveal character, shift a relationship), usually with a character wanting something and friction (see story-structure).
- **Engages the senses** — vivid but selective detail (not exhaustive — a few sharp details beat a catalog).
- **Moves** — enters late, leaves early (start close to the interesting part, cut when the point's made). Don't linger.
- **Changes something** — the situation/emotion/knowledge is different at the scene's end than its start.

## Balance: Immersion vs Pace

The tension: showing immerses but slows; telling moves but distances. Great writing balances them — dense sensory scenes for peaks, brisk summary for valleys — so the reader is immersed *where it counts* and carried swiftly elsewhere. Over-showing = bloated and slow; over-telling = flat and distant.

Write vividly by **showing important, emotional moments through concrete sensory detail, action, and body language** (letting readers infer and experience), while **telling/summarizing** the unimportant to keep pace — **choosing deliberately** what deserves a full scene. Ground scenes in place, give them a goal and tension, engage the senses selectively, and enter late/leave early. The art isn't "always show" — it's dramatizing what matters and trusting the reader to feel what you've made them see.
