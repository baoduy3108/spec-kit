# Cognitive Biases & Decision-Making

Human judgment relies on mental shortcuts that are usually efficient but produce **systematic, predictable errors** — cognitive biases. Understanding them improves your own decisions, helps you interpret others' behavior, and is essential for designing systems, research, and products that account for how people actually think (see behavioral-economics).

## Two Systems of Thinking

Kahneman's framework (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*):
- **System 1** — fast, automatic, intuitive, effortless, emotional. Handles most of life via pattern-matching and heuristics. Fast but bias-prone.
- **System 2** — slow, deliberate, analytical, effortful. Handles complex reasoning but is lazy and easily fatigued (limited attention/willpower).

Most biases arise when **System 1's shortcuts fire** where careful System 2 reasoning was needed, and System 2 fails to catch it. Knowing when to *engage* System 2 (high-stakes, unfamiliar, or bias-prone decisions) is a core skill.

## Heuristics

Biases mostly stem from **heuristics** — mental shortcuts that work well on average but misfire predictably:
- **Availability** — judging probability by how *easily examples come to mind* (overestimating vivid/recent risks — plane crashes over car crashes).
- **Representativeness** — judging by resemblance to a stereotype, ignoring base rates (the conjunction fallacy, "Linda problem").
- **Anchoring** — over-relying on the first number/value encountered, insufficiently adjusting away from it (huge in negotiation and estimation).
- **Affect** — letting current emotion drive judgments of risk/benefit.

## Common Biases

- **Confirmation bias** — seeking, favoring, and remembering information that confirms existing beliefs; ignoring disconfirming evidence. Perhaps the most pervasive and damaging.
- **Overconfidence** — systematically overestimating our knowledge, accuracy, and control (planning fallacy: underestimating time/cost; illusion of control).
- **Hindsight bias** — "I knew it all along" — seeing past events as more predictable than they were, distorting learning.
- **Loss aversion / sunk-cost fallacy** — throwing good resources after bad because of prior investment (see behavioral-economics).
- **Framing** — the same facts presented differently (90% survival vs 10% mortality) change decisions.
- **Fundamental attribution error** — attributing others' behavior to character, our own to circumstance.
- **Survivorship bias, base-rate neglect, recency bias, dunning-kruger, groupthink** — and dozens more, mostly variations on the same shortcut-gone-wrong theme.

## Why They Exist

Biases aren't stupidity — they're **adaptive shortcuts** that were efficient in ancestral environments and remain useful most of the time. The problem is a *mismatch* with modern statistical, abstract, and adversarial contexts. This reframing matters: everyone has them (intelligence doesn't immunize you), so humility and systems, not willpower, are the answer.

## Debiasing Strategies

You can't eliminate biases, but you can reduce their damage:
- **Awareness is necessary but insufficient** — knowing about a bias rarely stops it in the moment. Structural fixes work better.
- **Consider the opposite / seek disconfirming evidence** — actively argue against your view (counters confirmation bias).
- **Outside view / reference class** — base estimates on similar past cases and base rates, not the specific story (counters planning fallacy, representativeness).
- **Pre-mortems and checklists** — imagine the decision failed and ask why; use structured processes to force System 2.
- **Decompose and quantify** — break judgments into parts, use data and explicit probabilities.
- **Diverse input and red teams** — others catch biases you can't see in yourself (counters groupthink, overconfidence).
- **Delay and sleep on it** — reduce affect-driven and impulsive errors.

Improve decisions by understanding **System 1 (fast, biased) vs System 2 (slow, effortful)**, recognizing the **heuristics** (availability, anchoring, representativeness) and **biases** (confirmation, overconfidence, hindsight, framing) that systematically distort judgment, accepting they're **universal adaptive shortcuts**, and applying **structural debiasing** (consider the opposite, outside view, checklists, diverse input, quantification) rather than relying on willpower or awareness alone.
