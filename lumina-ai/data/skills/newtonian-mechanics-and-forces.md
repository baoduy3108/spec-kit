# Newtonian Mechanics & Forces

Classical (Newtonian) mechanics describes how objects move under forces — the physics of everyday life, from a thrown ball to a car braking to planets orbiting. Built on three elegant laws, it's remarkably powerful and intuitive once you grasp that **forces cause changes in motion**, not motion itself.

## The Three Laws of Motion

- **1st law (Inertia)** — an object at rest stays at rest, and one in motion stays in uniform motion, *unless acted on by a net force*. Motion doesn't need a force to continue; only to *change*. (Counterintuitive: things stop because of friction, not because motion "runs out.")
- **2nd law (F = ma)** — net force equals mass times acceleration. The core equation: a given force accelerates a lighter object more. Acceleration is *proportional to net force, inversely to mass*, and in the force's direction.
- **3rd law (Action-Reaction)** — for every force, there's an equal and opposite force on the other body. You push the ground back to walk; the rocket pushes exhaust down to go up. Forces always come in pairs (on *different* objects).

## Forces

Common forces to identify:
- **Gravity (weight)** — mass × g (≈9.8 m/s² near Earth), pulling down.
- **Normal force** — a surface pushing perpendicular against an object resting on it.
- **Friction** — resists relative sliding (static holds until a threshold; kinetic while sliding); proportional to normal force.
- **Tension** — pull through ropes/cables. **Applied, spring, drag** forces too.

The key skill is finding the **net force** (vector sum of all forces) — that's what F=ma uses.

## Free-Body Diagrams

The essential problem-solving tool: **isolate one object and draw every force acting on it** as an arrow (see statics-and-mechanics-of-materials). Get the FBD right — all forces, correct directions — and Newton's 2nd law (summing forces per axis) solves for unknowns. Most mechanics errors are FBD errors.

## Momentum & Impulse

- **Momentum** p = mv — "quantity of motion." **Conserved** in the absence of external forces: total momentum before = after (crucial for collisions).
- **Impulse** = force × time = change in momentum. A longer collision time reduces peak force (why airbags, crumple zones, and bending your knees on landing work).
- Collisions: **elastic** (kinetic energy conserved) vs **inelastic** (objects stick, KE lost to heat/deformation); momentum conserved in both.

## Work, Energy & Conservation

- **Work** = force × distance (in the force's direction); transfers energy.
- **Kinetic energy** = ½mv²; **potential energy** (gravitational mgh, spring ½kx²) is stored.
- **Conservation of energy** — energy transforms but isn't created/destroyed. Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved without friction; friction converts it to heat. This principle solves many problems more easily than force analysis (no need to track forces through the motion).

## Circular Motion

- Objects moving in a circle are **accelerating** (velocity direction changes), requiring a **centripetal force** directed *inward* (v²/r × m). Provided by tension, gravity, friction, or normal force depending on the setup.
- "Centrifugal force" is a fiction of the rotating frame — really it's inertia trying to go straight while a centripetal force curves the path.

Reason about motion with **Newton's three laws** (inertia; F=ma; action-reaction), by identifying **forces** and drawing **free-body diagrams** to find net force, using **momentum/impulse** (conserved; force × time) for collisions and impacts, applying **work-energy and conservation of energy** as a powerful shortcut, and treating **circular motion** as requiring an inward centripetal force. Master this and you can predict how virtually any everyday object moves.
