# Immersive Sim & Systemic Design

An immersive sim (Deus Ex, Dishonored, Prey, Thief) is built on a philosophy: give the player **consistent interacting systems and multiple tools**, then let *them* decide how to solve problems. Instead of scripting one intended path, the designer builds a rule-consistent world and trusts emergence. It's the purest expression of **player agency** in games.

## Systems Over Scripts

The defining principle: **simulate systems, don't script events**.
- A scripted game says "at this trigger, X happens." A systemic game says "these rules always hold, wherever they apply" — fire burns anything flammable, water conducts electricity, guards react to sound and sight, keys open any matching lock.
- Because rules are **consistent and universal**, they **combine** in ways the designer didn't explicitly author. That combination *is* the gameplay.

This is harder to build (systems must be robust everywhere, not faked in one spot) but yields depth no amount of scripting matches.

## Multiple Solutions & Player Expression

Every obstacle should have **many valid answers**, expressing different playstyles:
- A guarded door: pick the lock, hack it, find another route (vent, window), sneak past, knock out the guard, go in loud, or find a key. All should *work*.
- This respects player creativity and identity — the stealthy ghost, the hacker, the aggressive brute all thrive. Players feel the world responds to *their* idea, not the designer's.
- **"The player is right"** — if a plan is clever and consistent with the rules, it should succeed, even unforeseen. Punishing creativity kills the genre.

## Consistent World Rules

The magic depends on **trustworthy consistency**:
- Mechanics work the same everywhere (no arbitrary "this fire doesn't spread here").
- The player can *reason* about the world and predict outcomes, enabling planning and clever exploits.
- Consistency is what lets emergence be *fair* rather than random. Breaking your own rules breaks player trust and the whole illusion of a real place.

## Emergent Gameplay

**Emergence** — complex, unscripted situations arising from simple interacting rules — is the payoff. A guard's torch ignites spilled oil; a lured enemy triggers a trap meant for you; stacking crates makes an unintended climb. These player-authored moments are memorable *because* nobody designed them specifically. Systemic depth = near-infinite situations from finite rules (see open-world-and-sandbox-design).

## Reactivity & Agency

- **The world reacts** — to your choices, methods, and consequences (a knocked-out vs killed guard, an alerted vs oblivious level, NPCs remembering). Reactivity makes agency *matter*.
- **Meaningful consequence** — choices ripple, sometimes with no clean "right" answer, deepening immersion and replay.
- Players should feel like **authors of their playthrough**, not passengers on a ride.

## Design Discipline

- **Build robust systems first**, content second — invest in mechanics that generalize.
- **Anticipate combinations** — playtest for what players *do*, not what you intended; embrace clever exploits as features where possible.
- **Layered levels** — spaces designed with multiple routes/approaches in mind (verticality, alternates, interactable systems).
- **Restraint on hand-holding** — trust the player; over-guiding defeats the point.
- **Accept messiness** — emergence means less control; some player solutions will surprise (and occasionally break) you. That unpredictability is the feature.

Design immersive sims by **building consistent, interacting systems and handing players many tools**, then honoring whatever creative, rule-consistent solution they invent. The game becomes a space of possibility where each player writes their own story — the deepest form of agency in the medium.
