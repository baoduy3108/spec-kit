# Population Genetics & Evolution (Advanced Biology)

Population genetics studies **how allele frequencies change in populations** — the mathematical, quantitative core of evolution (see evolution-and-natural-selection). It's an advanced/gifted-exam (chuyên) biology topic, centered on the Hardy-Weinberg principle and the forces that drive genetic change.

## Populations & Allele Frequencies

- Evolution acts on **populations** (groups of interbreeding individuals sharing a **gene pool**), not individuals.
- **Allele frequency** — the proportion of a particular allele in the gene pool (e.g. p for a dominant allele A, q for recessive a, with p + q = 1).
- **Genotype frequencies** — proportions of AA, Aa, aa. Evolution is measured as *changes in these frequencies over generations*.

## The Hardy-Weinberg Principle

A foundational model — the **null hypothesis** of evolution:
- **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium** — in an *idealized* population, allele and genotype frequencies stay **constant** across generations. Genotype frequencies: **p² + 2pq + q² = 1** (AA + Aa + aa).
- **Conditions** (no evolution) — no selection, no mutation, no migration (gene flow), infinite population (no drift), and random mating. If all hold, frequencies don't change.
- **The power** — real populations *violate* these conditions, so **deviation from Hardy-Weinberg reveals that evolution is occurring** and which force is acting. It's the baseline against which change is detected.
- **Calculations** — given one frequency (e.g. q² = recessive phenotype fraction), compute p, q, and all genotype frequencies. A classic exam problem.

## Evolutionary Forces (Causes of Change)

When Hardy-Weinberg conditions break, allele frequencies shift:
- **Natural selection** — differential survival/reproduction favors some alleles (the main adaptive force). Can be directional, stabilizing, or disruptive.
- **Genetic drift** — random change in frequencies, strong in **small populations** (chance events; founder effect, bottleneck). Non-adaptive, can lose or fix alleles by luck.
- **Gene flow (migration)** — alleles moving between populations via migrants; tends to homogenize populations.
- **Mutation** — the ultimate source of *new* alleles (slow, but the raw material for all variation).
- **Non-random mating** — (e.g. assortative mating) changes genotype frequencies.

## Speciation

- When populations diverge enough (via these forces) and become **reproductively isolated**, they form new **species** — evolution at the population level scaling up.
- **Genetic distance** accumulates until interbreeding is impossible.

Master population genetics via **allele and genotype frequencies** in a gene pool (evolution = frequency change), the **Hardy-Weinberg principle** (p² + 2pq + q² = 1; equilibrium under 5 conditions; deviation reveals evolution — and enables calculations), and the **evolutionary forces** (selection, genetic drift, gene flow, mutation, non-random mating) that shift frequencies, driving **speciation**. Treating evolution quantitatively — as measurable changes in a population's genetics against the Hardy-Weinberg baseline — is the analytical depth that gifted-exam biology demands.
