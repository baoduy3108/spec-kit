# Worldbuilding for Fiction

Worldbuilding is creating the **setting** a story lives in — its geography, cultures, history, rules, and feel. A rich, believable world makes fiction immersive and can generate story itself. But worldbuilding serves the *story*, not the other way around: the craft is building enough to feel real, revealing it artfully, and never letting it smother the narrative.

## Internal Consistency (The Foundation)

The cardinal rule: a world must be **internally consistent**. Readers will accept *any* premise (dragons, FTL travel, talking cats) if the world follows its *own* rules reliably. Consistency creates believability and lets readers reason about the world (and enables fair plotting). **Breaking your own established rules** shatters immersion and trust more than any implausible premise. Decide the rules, then honor them.

## The Iceberg Principle

**Know far more than you show.** Like an iceberg, only ~10% of your worldbuilding should appear on the page; the other 90% exists to give what's shown *depth and authority*.
- A world where the author clearly knows the history, economy, and logic *beneath* the surface *feels* real, even when most stays implied. Readers sense the solid ground.
- But **don't dump the other 90%** on the reader (see infodumps below). The unseen mass supports the visible tip; it doesn't need to be displayed.

Build deep, reveal shallow.

## World in Service of Story

Worldbuilding must **serve the narrative**, not become an end in itself:
- **Relevant detail** — flesh out what the story touches; don't exhaustively detail every kingdom's tax code if it never matters. Depth where the story goes.
- **World generates story** — the best settings *create* conflict and plot (a rigid caste system, a dying resource, a magic with a terrible cost). Build tensions into the world.
- Beware "worldbuilder's disease" — endless creation that never becomes a *story*. The map and lore are scaffolding for a tale, not the tale.

## Systems: Magic, Technology, Rules

- **Magic/tech systems** — decide their **rules, limits, and costs**. "Hard" systems (clear, consistent rules — magic as engineering) enable the magic to *solve* problems satisfyingly (see Sanderson's laws). "Soft" systems (mysterious, wondrous) shouldn't conveniently solve plot problems (that's deus ex machina). Either way: **limits and costs create tension and prevent the magic from trivializing conflict.**
- **Consequences** — powers/tech should have trade-offs and shape the society around them (how would this world *actually* work with this magic?).

## Culture, History & Geography

A living world has interlocking elements:
- **Geography** shapes cultures, economies, and conflicts (mountains isolate, rivers connect, resources drive war).
- **History** — the past explains the present; events, grudges, and legacies give depth and motivation. A world with history feels *lived-in*.
- **Culture** — believable societies with their own values, customs, religions, languages, and internal diversity (not monolithic "the elf culture"). Cultures should feel like they emerged from their conditions.
- **Interconnection** — these elements should *cause* each other (this geography → this economy → this conflict), creating a coherent, plausible whole.

## Revealing the World (Avoiding Infodumps)

*How* you convey the world matters as much as the world itself:
- **Avoid infodumps** — big blocks of exposition/lore stop the story cold and bore readers. Resist the urge to explain everything.
- **Reveal organically** — through action, dialogue, character experience, and telling details (see show-dont-tell). Drop the reader *in* and let them absorb the world by living it (immersive "in medias res"), trusting them to piece it together.
- **The telling detail** — one specific, evocative detail implies a vast world (a customs officer bored of dragon inspections tells you dragons are common and bureaucratized). Imply the iceberg through glimpses.
- **Just-in-time** — reveal what the reader needs *when* they need it, woven into the moment, not front-loaded.

Build fictional worlds that are **internally consistent** (honor your own rules), **deep beneath but shallow on the surface** (the iceberg), **in service of story** (generating conflict, detailed where it matters), with **systems whose limits and costs create tension**, **interconnected culture/history/geography** that feel caused and lived-in — all revealed **organically through detail and experience**, never infodumps. The goal: a world so believable and alive that readers forget it's invented, discovered through the story rather than lectured about.
