# Writing Well in French

French is an inflected Romance language prized for **precision and elegance**, with a strong prescriptive tradition (the Académie française). Writing well means mastering **grammatical gender and agreement**, the **tu/vous** politeness distinction, a rich verb-conjugation system, and the accents that are integral to spelling — all while achieving natural French flow.

## Grammatical Gender & Agreement

Every French noun is **masculine or feminine** (le/la, un/une) — often unpredictably (le livre, la table). This gender propagates through **agreement**:
- **Adjectives agree** in gender and number (un grand homme / une grande femme / des grandes femmes).
- **Articles, past participles** (with être, and with a preceding direct object), and pronouns agree.
Getting gender and agreement right is fundamental; errors are immediately noticeable. Learning each noun *with* its gender is essential.

## Tu vs Vous (Formality)

French has two "you": **tu** (informal, singular — friends, family, children, peers) and **vous** (formal singular, and all plural — strangers, elders, superiors, respect). Choosing wrong is a real social error (tu to a stranger is presumptuous; vous to a close friend is cold). When unsure, start with **vous**. This T-V distinction runs through the whole language.

## Verb Conjugation & Tenses

French verbs conjugate extensively by person, number, tense, and mood:
- **Many tenses/moods** — présent, imparfait, passé composé, futur, conditionnel, subjonctif, etc. The **subjunctive** (used after certain expressions of doubt/emotion/necessity) is a notable challenge.
- **Passé composé vs imparfait** — a key distinction (completed action vs ongoing/habitual past) that English speakers struggle with.
- Three verb groups (-er, -ir, -re) plus many irregulars. Correct conjugation is core literacy.

## Accents & Spelling

Accents (é è ê ë à â ç ù î ï ô) are **part of the spelling**, not optional decoration — they distinguish words (ou "or" vs où "where"; a "has" vs à "to") and pronunciation. French spelling is notoriously non-phonetic (many silent letters); precision matters. The **cédille (ç)** and **liaisons** are part of the system.

## Register & Elegance

- **Register** — French has a pronounced formal/informal split. Formal French uses fuller constructions, the subjunctive, elevated vocabulary, and (in very formal writing) the passé simple; casual French uses on for "we," dropped ne in negation (spoken), verlan slang, and contractions.
- **Elegance** — French style values clarity, balance, and a certain refinement; well-constructed sentences and precise word choice are esteemed. Politeness formulas are elaborate (letter closings, "je vous prie d'agréer…").

## False Friends & Natural Phrasing

- **Faux amis (false friends)** — words that look English but differ: *actuellement* (currently, not "actually"), *librairie* (bookshop, not "library"), *sensible* (sensitive), *assister à* (to attend). A classic trap.
- **Natural phrasing** — French prefers certain constructions (nominal style, specific prepositions, idiomatic expressions); literal English→French sounds anglicized. Use natural French idiom and structure.

Write French well by mastering **grammatical gender and thoroughgoing agreement**, choosing **tu vs vous** correctly for the relationship, conjugating verbs accurately across **tenses and the subjunctive**, spelling with correct **accents** (they're part of the word), matching **register** (formal French's elegance and subjunctive vs casual), and avoiding **false friends** and anglicisms for natural phrasing. Good French is precise, correctly-agreed, appropriately formal, and elegantly turned.
